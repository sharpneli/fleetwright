# What a warship's electric plant fed, 1890-1945

Status: draft    Updated: 2026-11-04    Request: [REQUESTS.md, 2026-11-04](../REQUESTS.md)

## Summary

A warship's electric load was mostly motors, not lamps. The only two period load tables by consumer found are from 1900-02. In Kearsarge's 655 kW, turret training, elevation and rammers plus ammunition hoists were 44%, boat cranes and winches 33%, fans 15%, lighting 7% and searchlights about 1%. In Kaiser Friedrich III's 221 kW, hoists were 35%, searchlights 25%, lighting 17% and fans 13%.

How much a ship drew followed from what she ran on electricity, not from her date. USN, German, French and Russian turrets were electric or electro-hydraulic from about 1897-1909. RN heavy turrets ran on steam-driven hydraulic pumping engines from the 1890s to Nelson (1927) and likely KGV. An electric trip therefore stopped a German or American turret's drives, and left a British one training on hydraulics with its lights, fans, director pointers and secondary hoists gone.

The plants show the same split. In 1909-16, German dreadnoughts had 60-90 W per tonne (Nassau 1,280 kW, König 2,040, Bayern 2,400). RN ships had 20-40 W/t (Dreadnought 300-400 kW, Tiger and Renown 750). WWII ships ranged from 70 W/t (KGV 2,640 kW, Yamato 4,800) to 190-260 W/t (Bismarck 7,910, Iowa 10,000).

USN designers sized the plant at about half the connected load. At battle duty the summed load came near the plant rating. No WWII battle-load analysis was found.

When power went, most consumers stopped dead. Fire-control tables went to hand cranks with one more man. Director receivers froze, and guns went to local laying. Gyros lost their repeaters at once and needed minutes to hours to settle again. USN power turrets set their brakes and stood still. Arcs went out. Steam-driven pumps, steering engines and turret pumps carried on.

Fuses protected branch circuits and breakers protected switchboards. The RN's first ring main with section breakers was Defence (1908), and its breakers still discriminated poorly in 1938. No ship-wide loss of power from above-water shellfire before 1925 was found. Every ship-wide loss came from water in the dynamo or switchboard spaces (Lion 1915, Szent István 1918, Audacious 1914), from an underwater explosion cutting the ring main (Inflexible 1915), or from a ship's last minutes (Blücher). Shell hits gave concussion trips, mended in minutes, and severed circuits in one space.

### 2. The battle load ran near the plant; the connected load ran at twice it (USN)

No WWII battle-against-cruising analysis was reached. The evidence is three period lists, all from before 1912.

**Kearsarge, 1900: 655 kW connected on a 350 kW plant** (George 1902).

| Group | W | Share | Battle duty [EST] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boat cranes and winches | 216,350 | 33.0% | off |
| Training, elevating and rammer motors | 171,580 | 26.2% | on, intermittent |
| Ammunition hoists and whips | 115,138 | 17.6% | on, intermittent |
| Ventilating motors and portable fans | 98,000 | 15.0% | on |
| 700 incandescent lamps | 48,000 | 7.3% | on |
| 4 × 30-in searchlights | 6,000 | 0.9% [UNCERTAIN: the paper's own currents give ~6 kW per light] | night |

The battle groups sum to 433-457 kW. Gun machinery is intermittent, so a diversity of 0.6-0.7 on it brings the battle load to about 300-350 kW. That is 0.85-1.0 of the plant [EST].

**Kaiser Friedrich III, c.1900: 221 kW of loads on a 324 kW plant** (George 1902).

| Group | W | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Ammunition hoists | 78,440 | 35.4% |
| Searchlights | 55,500 | 25.1% |
| Lighting | 38,260 | 17.3% |
| Ventilation | 28,340 | 12.8% |
| Gun training | 8,880 | 4.0% |
| Coal winches | 7,400 | 3.3% |
| Refrigeration | 4,500 | 2.0% |

A night-battle load (everything but winches and refrigeration) is 209 kW, or 0.65 of the plant. By day it is about 0.47 [EST]. German practice sized the plant above the summed load; USN practice sized it at about half of connected. The small gun-training line suggests her turrets trained mostly by hand or hydraulics [EST].

**A USN battleship c.1911 on 4 × 300 kW** (USNI 1911). Connected motors per group [EST sums]:

| Group | Nameplate | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| Turret machinery | ≈1,240 hp | 12 each of hoists 40 hp, breech 3½, rammers 10, turning 35, elevating 15 |
| Forced-draught blowers | 540 hp | 12 × 45 hp |
| Galley | ≈590 hp | ranges "190 HP and 380 HP" [UNCERTAIN wording], 20 hp oven |
| Handling | ≈470 hp | 2 boat-crane hoists × 40, 2 slew × 30, 7 winches × 35, elevators, chain hoists, conveyors |
| Pumps, shops, jacking | ≈100 hp | |
| Communications | ≈7 kW | wireless 5 kW, IC 4 × 500 W |

The total is about 2,950 hp, or 2,200 kW plus fans, lamps and searchlights. That makes plant/connected about 0.55, the same as Kearsarge.

At battle duty, take turrets × 0.5, forced draught × 0.9, fans, lamps and lights about 150 kW, and the galley and handling off. That gives about 1,100 kW, or 0.9 of the 1,200 kW plant [EST].

**Design rules of the period.** Generators were to carry a 33⅓% overload for two hours. Motor feeders were sized separately for continuous and intermittent duty (George 1902). In 1906 one 24 kW set was matched to four 30-in lights burning together (USNI 1906). Two generators were kept running at all times while an essential motor was on the line (USNI 1918).

The modern USN method (DDS 310-1, 2012) multiplies each consumer's connected kW by a load factor per condition: anchor, cruising, functional (battle) and emergency. It uses 0.9 for a motor at full load continuously and demand factors of 0.45-0.95 by group. The WWII procedure was not found.

**Emergency plant.** WWII USN emergency diesels were 4-5% of the ship's-service plant [EST]: 400/8,400 kW, 400/7,000 and 500/10,000. They fed steering first (Kidd). German diesels were a third to all of the plant from 1912: Bayern all-diesel, Bismarck 4,000 of 7,910 kW.

The training motors fall on one line [EST]:

**P_train (kW drawn, installed) ≈ 0.035 × W_rev (t) × ω_train (°/s)**

Per-ship values of the constant:

| Turret | Constant |
|---|---|
| New York | 0.026 |
| Nevada | 0.035 |
| Pennsylvania | 0.037 |
| Colorado | 0.025 |
| KGV twin | 0.034 |
| Iowa | 0.040 |
| KGV quad | 0.046 |
| North Carolina | 0.048 |
| Yamato (outlier) | 0.09 |

The game's own friction model, P = μ·m·g·r·ω with μ = 0.007 and roller radii of 4.3-6 m [EST], gives friction power of 4.4 kW for New York, 46 kW for Iowa and 23 kW for the KGV quad. The installed motors are **4.2-5.2 × that friction power**. The margin covers acceleration, list, wind and gear losses.

Elevation per gun fits [EST]:

**P_elev (hp per gun) ≈ 0.0014 × d³ × ω_elev**

d is the calibre in inches and ω the elevation rate in °/s. The fit gives 15 hp for New York (stated 15), 46 for Colorado (stated 50) and 69 for Iowa (stated 60), and predicts 8-10 hp for a 12-in at 4°/s (stated 8 in 1907). Nevada's twin, at 30 hp, sits twice above the line.

Whole-turret connected load:
- About **0.25-0.4 kW per revolving tonne** for 1900-1921 turrets: 1.5-2°/s, 4-8°/s, 1.25-1.75 rpm (Kearsarge 0.32, New York 0.26, Colorado 0.28-0.42).
- About **0.7 kW/t** for the 1940s US turrets: 4°/s, 12°/s, 2 rpm (North Carolina 0.73, Iowa 0.67).

Battle load factor: 0.4-0.6 of connected. Training, elevation and one loading step run at once, and a Waterbury pump idles unloaded at zero stroke [EST from appendix b, appendix i and George's 0.6-0.7 diversity].

German 30.5 cm turrets (534-549 t, 3°/s train, 4°/s elevation, about 20 s a round) trained electrically. Elevation, rammer and breech were hydraulic from two electrically driven triple pumps per turret. No kW was found. By the formula, training is about 55 kW installed [EST].

- Battle load factor 0.6-0.8. Princess Royal switched off her upper and main deck lighting before Jutland against fire from shorted leads.

**Do the RN pumping engines care about an electric trip?** For Nelson, no: the pumping engines are steam (sourced). For WWI ships the drive of the pumping engines is not stated in any source reached. The circumstantial case for steam is strong:
- Hansard 1901 says the turrets were not worked by electricity.
- Audacious's auxiliaries were "almost entirely steam powered".
- Marlborough kept firing after a lower power board trip.
- Inflexible's mine severed the ring main and put out every light while "steam held".
- WWI plants of 750 kW could not have run five turrets' training and loading at the US rate of 130-375 kW a turret.

So treat WWI RN heavy turrets as not on the electric plant [EST, high confidence]. Their electric parts were lighting, turret fans, director receivers, firing circuits (supply unconfirmed) and telephones.

**What each drive gained** (all sourced to navweaps, USNI 2023 and appendix b unless marked).

Training and elevation rates by drive:

| Drive | Training °/s | Elevation °/s |
|---|---|---|
| Hydraulic | 2 (15-in Mk I); 4, or 2 when rolling > 5° (Nelson); 2 (KGV) | 5-10 |
| Direct electric | 2.4-3.0 (Russian 1895); full traverse < 45 s (Kearsarge); 6 (Littorio) | 2.2-2.7 (Russian); 6 (Littorio) |
| Electric training with hydraulic elevation (German WWI) | 3 | 4 |
| Waterbury | 1.7-2 (1914-21); 4 (1941) | 4-8 (1914-21); 12 (1941) |

Reload is set by the loading arrangement more than by the drive:
- RN 15-in: 32-36 s.
- German 30.5 cm: about 20 s.
- USN 14-in: 34-48 s.
- Iowa: 30 s.
- Russian 12-in in 1904-05: 0.5-0.7 rpm.

Each drive's strengths and weaknesses:
- Hydraulics gave smooth, strong low-speed control and kept turret power off the electric plant. Their walking pipes leaked, and from Nelson the oil was a fire risk.
- Direct DC electric was compact and pipe-free. Its speed control was crude, it tripped breakers when starting under load, and its sparks in the gunhouse caused fires.
- The Waterbury gear gave hydraulic smoothness from an electric source, at high machining cost.

No drive-machinery weights were found for any type.

## Recommendations for this codebase

The game rule holds on the record: what a ship loses with her power is what she runs on it. British and German capital ships at Jutland both had plants of 750-2,040 kW, but only the German turrets stood on theirs. Every recommendation below sets the danger from what the design carries. None uses the date.

### EL6: the trip rate from the battle load

1. **The rates per hit are consistent with the record.**
   - Ship-wide 0.005 per heavy hit: the record's 0 in about 100 hits only bounds it at ≤ 0.03.
   - Section 0.08: the record gives about 0.04 (0.01-0.10). Halve it, or keep it as within the band.
   - Local 0.40: the record gives 0.3-0.5.
2. **The anchor loads are misplaced.** "WWI ~300 kW" is a pre-dreadnought's battle load: Kearsarge about 300-350 kW, Kaiser Friedrich III about 210. The ships behind the WWI rates had 750-2,040 kW plants and battle loads of roughly 300-1,000 kW [EST].

   Proposed anchors on log battle kW, interpolated and clamped [EST]:

   | Battle load | Ship-wide | Section | Local |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | 100 kW | 0 | 0.02 | 0.25 |
   | 300 kW | 0.002 | 0.04 | 0.30 |
   | 1,000 kW | 0.005 | 0.06 | 0.40 |
   | 5,000 kW | 0.02 | 0.15 | 0.50 |

   This drops the pre-1914 anchor as a date. A Borodino, at 738 kW with electric turrets, lands near the WWI rates. Tsushima's frequent turret electrical faults bear that out locally.
3. The WWII anchor (0.02, 0.15, 0.50 at several MW) was not tested by this research. The WWII cases found are torpedoes and mines: Prince of Wales, Ark Royal and Belfast.
4. **Ship-wide loss is a water and underwater-explosion event, not a shell-shock roll.** Lion, Audacious, Inflexible, Szent István and Prince of Wales all lost the plant through flooding of dynamo, switchboard or boiler spaces, or through an underwater blast severing the ring main. Put that in EL7: plant out when its space floods. Add a ship-wide cascade on torpedo and mine shock at a far higher rate than shells [EST: Inflexible, Marlborough, Seydlitz and Szent István are 4 of 4 underwater hits with a section or ship-wide effect]. Keep the shell shock roll low.
5. `CascadeMin`: 90% at 1-5 min fits the concussion trips (Marlborough "very quickly", Seydlitz re-switched, Argo 10 min). Widen the 10% tail to 5-45 min for Lützow's 45 min [EST]. A flooded generating group stays out until pumped dry or until port (Lion).
6. Local losses in the record are mostly severed leads, not trips. They last for the action, not minutes: Warspite's TCT, Princess Royal's turret flat and 4-in circuits. Make local outcomes split between "tripped" (minutes) and "cut" (until repaired by a party) [EST: half and half].
7. Add a "darken ship" readiness option: Princess Royal cut her upper and main deck lighting before action. It removes lighting load in those spaces and the short-circuit fire risk.

### EL7: generators as design facts

1. Sets: count, kW, prime mover (reciprocating, turbo, diesel) and space. Recorded mixes:
   - 3-8 sets for 1906-1920 (Dreadnought 3-4 × 100; Renown 2 recip. + 1 turbo + 1 oil; Hood 8 of three kinds; König 4 turbo + 2 diesel).
   - 6-14 sets for WWII.
   - Groups in 2-4 spaces (Seydlitz forward and aft; Bismarck four plants; Iowa per engine room).
2. Capacity from load:
   - Plant ≈ 1.0-1.5 × battle load. That is 1.5 for Kaiser Friedrich III and about 1.0-1.2 for Kearsarge and the c.1911 USN ship.
   - Plant ≈ 0.5-0.55 × connected for USN practice.
   - Overload 1.33 for 2 h (George 1902). Richelieu's 1,250 kW five-minute rating is [UNCERTAIN].
3. Emergency diesels: 4-5% of plant (WWII USN). They feed steering first (Kidd), then fire control and pumps.
4. Steam draw: a 1906 100 kW reciprocating set used 27.5 lb of water per kWh (12.5 kg/kWh, USNI 1907). Generator steam is a boiler load. Diesels survive the loss of boiler rooms, as Lützow and Szent István show.
5. Partial loss sheds load:
   - Seydlitz ran on two thirds of her supply.
   - Szent István kept lights over pumps.
   - WWII practice gave automatic changeover to vital services (MacLaren).

   Shed in this order: cranes and galley; then searchlights by day; then pumps not fighting damage; then hoists; last steering, fire control and lighting [EST].
6. Weight: diesel sets were heavier than turbo sets of equal capacity (Laudahn, qualitative). No per-kW weights were found.

### Checks (benches and tests) with bands

1. `shipgen electrics` on a USN pre-dreadnought with electric turrets: connected 550-750 kW, battle 0.8-1.25 × plant (Kearsarge: 655 connected, 350 plant).
2. On a German pre-dreadnought: battle 0.5-0.8 × plant (Kaiser Friedrich III: 209 kW at night on 324 kW).
3. On an RN 1914-16 battleship or battlecruiser with steam-hydraulic turrets: battle 0.3-0.8 × a 750 kW plant [EST]. Turrets contribute 0 kW.
4. On a German 1912-16 dreadnought: battle 0.3-0.7 × 1,660-2,400 kW [EST].
5. On a WWII fast battleship: battle 0.4-0.8 × plant [EST; no load analysis found]. Emergency diesel 3-6% of plant.
6. Per turret, connected: Iowa Mk 7 1,000-1,250 kW; New York 14-in 110-150 kW; Colorado 250-380 kW.
7. Training check: installed training kW ÷ (μ·m·g·r·ω at μ = 0.007) between 3.5 and 6 for every power-driven heavy turret.
8. Searchlight: 30-in at 80 V 5.7 kW ± 10%; 60-in at 125 V 25 kW ± 10%.
9. Lighting: 3-6 W/t on carbon lamps.
10. `damage-history` on the Jutland and Dogger Bank cases (Lion, Derfflinger, Warspite, Princess Royal, Seydlitz, Lützow):
    - ship-wide power losses from above-water shellfire ≤ 2 in the panel's ~100 heavy hits;
    - section losses 1-10;
    - most outages under 5 min, with a tail to about 45 min.
11. A flooded switchboard or dynamo space blacks the ship out (Lion). A flooded boiler room in a ship with diesel sets keeps the lights (Szent István).
12. Test: an RN 1916 design on steam-hydraulic turrets trains at the same rate before and after an electric trip. A German 1916 design's turrets stop or fall to hand.
