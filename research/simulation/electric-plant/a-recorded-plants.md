## Q1. Recorded plants by ship, 1890-1945

### Takeaway
A capital ship's plant grew from about 50-100 kW (c.1890), to 350-750 kW (c.1900-04), to 1,200-2,400 kW (dreadnoughts of 1909-1916), to 4,000-10,000 kW (1939-45 battleships). Voltage went from 67-80 V DC, to 105-125 V DC (c.1900-1910), to 220-225 V DC (German from 1909, and most WWII European/Japanese ships), to 450 V 3-phase AC (USN from the late 1930s).

Three of the brief's "already known" figures need correcting or qualifying:
- **Iowa** had **8** × 1,250 kW, not 4: a pair in each engine room, 10,000 kW at 450 V AC, plus 2 × 250 kW emergency diesels.
- **Bismarck's** 7,910 kW is confirmed: 8 × 500 kW diesel + 5 × 690 kW + 1 × 460 kW turbo, at 220 V.
- **Dreadnought** had 3 or 4 × 100 kW at 100 V DC [UNCERTAIN].

### Inferences
- Kaiser Friedrich III's "five 320 kW 74 volt generators" is almost certainly a total of 320 kW over five dynamos [EST]. 320 kW at 74 V would be about 4,300 A per machine. US standard sets of 1893-1907 were 8-100 kW, and Brandenburg (1893) had 73-97 kW in all, so that reading is implausible. "Four 240 kW" for the sister ships is the same case. Deutschland's "four turbo generators that provided 260 kW each" (1,040 kW) is plausible against Nassau's 1,280 kW three years later, but "260 kW in all" cannot be excluded [UNCERTAIN].
- Dreadnought's 300-400 kW stands out as low beside contemporary German and Russian ships: Pobeda 555 kW (1902), Borodino 738 kW (1904), Nassau 1,280 kW (1909). That fits the RN's reputation for hydraulic rather than electric turret machinery [EST].
- USN ships before the late 1930s were DC, at 80 V in 1893 and 125 V by 1907-1911, with 240 V DC busses by 1919 (Tennessee). The switch to 450 V AC came with North Carolina and South Dakota. German ships went to 220-225 V DC in 1909 (Nassau) and stayed there through Bismarck. Yamato was also 220 V DC (single source).
- A rough shape for a model: about 0.01 kW/t (1893), 0.03-0.05 (1900-04), 0.06-0.09 (German dreadnoughts), 0.02 (Dreadnought), 0.13-0.26 (1939-45) [EST; see Q3].

## Q2. Published load analyses and consumer breakdowns; capacity against load

### Takeaway
No true naval battle-against-cruising load analysis (kW by consumer group) turned up. The best period evidence is of two kinds:
- **Connected load against plant.** Kearsarge/Kentucky had 661 kW of appliances on 350 kW of plant (plant = 0.53 of connected). A c.1911 USN battleship lists its motors by horsepower against 4 × 300 kW of plant.
- **Merchant analogues.** A 1935 IMarE paper gives sea loads by consumer group for a liner and a cargo ship.

The pattern: designers sized the plant at about half the connected load, relying on diversity, since not every motor runs at once [EST].

### Cited Findings
- Kentucky and Kearsarge: "350 kilowatts generator capacity, and electrical appliances calling for 661 kilowatts". The appliances were turret motors, ammunition hoists, boat cranes, deck winches and ventilating motors, with no per-item kW. — [USNI Proceedings June 1919](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1919/june/electrical-division-aboard-ship)
- The same article lists the consumers Texas's watch ran from her plants: ventilating blowers, galley ranges and ovens, fresh-water and flushing pumps, turret and 5-in hoist motors, steering and forced-draught motors, searchlights, fire-control and interior-communication circuits. No loads are given. — [USNI 1919](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1919/june/electrical-division-aboard-ship)
- A c.1911 USN battleship on 4 × 300 kW. Motor list as the tool returned it:

- Richelieu ran the turbo-generators underway and the diesels in port, so the battle plant is the 6,000 kW turbo set, not the 9,000 kW total. — [Wikipedia, citing Jordan & Dumas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richelieu-class_battleship)

### Inferences
- **Connected load of the c.1911 battleship [EST].** Summing the listed motors (ranges taken as listed):

  | Group | Connected |
  |---|---|
  | Turret machinery | ≈1,240 hp |
  | Forced draught | 540 hp |
  | Galley | ≈590 hp |
  | Boat, coal and ammunition handling | ≈470 hp |
  | Pumps, shops, jacking | ≈100 hp |
  | **Total** | ≈2,950 hp ≈ 2,200 kW, plus unlisted fans, lighting and searchlights |

  That is against 1,200 kW of plant, so plant ≈ 0.5 of connected load, the same ratio as Kearsarge (0.53). By connected horsepower, turret machinery is about 40% and ventilation/forced draught about 20%+.
- These motors do not all run together. In battle the galley, winches, cranes and shops are off and turrets, hoists, forced draught and ventilation are on. So battle load plausibly sits near the plant rating, while cruising load is lighting, ventilation, galley and pumps [EST]. A per-consumer model summed at connected rating should therefore come out at about 1.5-2 × the recorded plant. A model summed at battle duty should come out near or below it.
- Check that "190 HP and 380 HP" ranges is the article's actual wording before using it. It may be watts or the tool's error.

## Q3. kW per ton and per man over time

### Takeaway

### Cited Findings
- The kW values are as cited in Q1. Displacements and complements are not sourced in this session; they are general reference values for the trend only [EST].

### Inferences
- Two clusters by navy [EST]:
  - **Electrified:** German ships (and US ships with electric turrets) ran 0.06-0.09 kW/t in 1909-16.
  - **Hydraulic:** RN ships ran about 0.02-0.04 kW/t, with hydraulic turret machinery and steam auxiliaries. Yamato and KGV (~0.07) stayed low into WWII, against 0.19-0.26 for Bismarck, Richelieu and the US fast battleships.
- A wargame model should therefore let navy doctrine (electric vs hydraulic turrets, steam vs electric auxiliaries) shift kW/t by roughly 2-3×, not only date.

### Gaps
- Displacements and complements were not fetched with sources this session. The writer should take them from standard references before publishing the ratios.

## Q4. Diesel and emergency generators, and batteries, by navy and date

### Takeaway
- **Germany** led with diesel ship's-service generators: Kaiser class 1912 (2 diesel of 6 sets), König 1914, Derfflinger 1914, and all-diesel Bayern 1916 (8 × 300 kW). The diesel generators then stayed in the German mix (Scharnhorst, Bismarck: 4,000 of 7,910 kW in diesels).
- **RN:** Dreadnought (1906) reportedly had Mirrlees diesel-driven dynamos (2 of 4 engines, later 1). Hood reportedly had 2 diesel of 8. But interwar RN cruisers (County to Dido/Colony) and the carrier Ark Royal were all-steam, which the Bucknill Committee called "a major design failure". Diesels were retrofitted afterwards.
- **USN:** WWII ships kept separate small emergency diesels (200-250 kW on battleships, 100 kW on destroyers). North Carolina also had 4 × 850 kW ship's-service diesels.
- **France:** Richelieu's diesels were the harbour plant, with 2 × 140 kW emergency diesels.

### Cited Findings
- Laudahn 1913/14 on German auxiliary diesels: Bayern all-diesel main generators; Sachsen's generator diesels diverted to U-boats; starting air for ten starts in succession; diesel sets "cannot compare favourably in the matter of weight with a turbo-dynamo of equal capacity". — [J. Nav. Eng. 31(3) 1989](https://library.imarest.org/record/8600/files/v31b3p17a.pdf)
- After Ark Royal's loss, the RN fitted diesel generators "in those ships where none existed". — [MacLaren 1961](https://library.imarest.org/record/9717/files/v13b1p07a.pdf); Bucknill quote via the existing project doc `04_carriers.md` (from [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Ark_Royal_(91)))
- Dreadnought: 2 Brotherhood steam + 2 Mirrlees diesel engines, later 3 steam + 1 diesel. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Dreadnought_(1906))
- The German classes' diesels — [König](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6nig-class_battleship), [Kaiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiser-class_battleship), [Derfflinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derfflinger-class_battlecruiser), [Bayern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayern-class_battleship), [Scharnhorst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scharnhorst-class_battleship), [Bismarck](https://www.kbismarck.com/propulsioni.html)
- US emergency diesels:
  - [North Carolina 2 × 200 kW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Carolina-class_battleship)
  - [South Dakota 2 × 200 kW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Dakota-class_battleship_(1939))
  - [Iowa 2 × 250 kW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iowa-class_battleship)
  - [Sumner 100 kW](https://dd-692.com/machinery.htm)
  - [Kidd 100 kW, steering first](https://www.usskidd.com/ship-tour/the-emergency-diesel-generator/)
- RN submarine (Porpoise class) batteries were connected to give 880 V, with 440 V for ordinary working. — [MacLaren 1961](https://library.imarest.org/record/9717/files/v13b1p07a.pdf)

### Inferences
- Emergency diesel capacity in USN WWII ships is about 4-5% of ship's-service capacity [EST: 400/8,400; 400/7,000; 500/10,000]. That is enough for steering, IC, fire control and some pumps, not turrets.
- German dreadnoughts' diesel share was about a third to all of the plant (Bayern), which makes them the most robust to the loss of steam [EST].

### Gaps
- Nothing found on ship storage batteries for IC, fire control or emergency lighting in surface ships, for any navy.
- Japanese, Italian and Russian emergency generators not found.
