## Searchlights: kW per light, numbers carried, arc current and voltage, battle load vs installed

### Takeaway
A searchlight arc burned at about 45-55 V whatever the mirror size. Size changed the current: about 50-65 A for a 60 cm light, 80-120 A for 90 cm, and 150 A for a 100 cm German Schuckert of the 1890s. The ship's 80 V (later 125 V) mains fed the arc through a series rheostat that burned off the voltage difference, so each light drew 4-6 kW from the plant around 1906. A USN source sized one 24 kW generator for four 30-inch lights burning together.

### Cited Findings
- The 1894 table, all lights at 45-55 V: 90 cm, 80-120 A, about 40,000 cp, 4.9-9.0 electric HP; 60 cm, 50-65 A, about 35,000 cp, 3.0-4.9 electric HP; 40 cm, 30-50 A, about 20,000 cp, 1.9-3.7 electric HP; 30 cm, 11-20 A, about 3,000 cp, 0.7-1.5 electric HP. — [USNI Proceedings 1894, "Electric Search Lights at Sea"](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1894/october/electric-search-lights-sea)
- Some German ships carried 100 cm Schuckert mirrors needing "150 amperes". — [USNI 1894](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1894/october/electric-search-lights-sea)
- Numbers carried in 1894: some navies allowed four, three or two searchlights on a battleship. Large Italian ships carried at least six: one in the top and five in deck ports. Torpedo and patrol boats carried the smallest sizes. — [USNI 1894](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1894/october/electric-search-lights-sea)
- Use in 1894 doctrine: a fleet under way kept its lights ready from sunset to dawn, with some running constantly. Cruisers swept synchronised one-minute sectors so beams didn't overlap. Battleships showed lights only when necessary. Two beams should not run on the same side of a ship during fleet movements. — [USNI 1894](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1894/october/electric-search-lights-sea)
- USN mirror sizes in 1906: 13, 18, 24 and 30 inch. "Two 36 in and one 60 in have been used" (trials only at that date). — [USNI Proceedings 1906, "Electrical Installations, United States Navy"](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1906/october/electrical-installations-united-states-navy)
- 24-inch projector rheostat example: 0.62 ohm, 50 A, 50 V at the lamp, 80 V on the line. The arc runs at "about 50 volts", kept "from 47 to 50 volts" for efficiency. — [USNI 1906](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1906/october/electrical-installations-united-states-navy)
- A 30-inch searchlight uses "5.7 kilowatts for each search-light". Four draw "nearly 23 kilowatts", about the output of a "24 k. w. generator". A motor-generator to save the rheostat loss was considered, but intermittent use "hardly warrants the extra device". — [USNI 1906](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1906/october/electrical-installations-united-states-navy)
- USN searchlights of 1906 were trained and elevated by electric motors (40° elevation, 20° depression, full traverse), with hand wheels and coupling nuts for hand working. — [USNI 1906](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1906/october/electrical-installations-united-states-navy)
- German WWII Army lights, for comparison: the 150 cm drew "200 amperes at 110 volts" (22 kW) from a 24 kW generator, about 990 million cd; the 60 cm Flak light ran from an 8 kW generator; the 200 cm from a 120 kW generator. The navy also used "a few obsolete 110 centimetre searchlights". — [Wikipedia, German searchlights of WWII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_searchlights_of_World_War_II)

### Inferences
- [EST] The current figures convert to kW. At the arc (50 V): 60 cm 2.5-3.3 kW, 90 cm 4-6 kW, 100 cm Schuckert about 7.5 kW. From an 80 V main through the rheostat (current × line voltage): 24-inch (≈60 cm) 4.0 kW, 30-inch 5.7 kW (cited), 90 cm 6.4-9.6 kW, 100 cm 12 kW. From a 125 V main the rheostat burned more still. For the game: plant kW ≈ arc current × mains voltage, not the arc's own power.
- [EST] By extrapolation from the German Army 150 cm (200 A, 22 kW) and the 30-inch's 5.7 kW: a 36-inch/90 cm high-intensity light of WWI-WWII is about 10-15 kW from the plant, a 110 cm about 15 kW, a 150 cm naval light about 20-25 kW. [UNCERTAIN] I found no naval source for the 36-inch, 110 cm or 150 cm current. My unverified recollection that the USN WWII 36-inch drew about 150 A needs checking.
- [EST] Battle load against installed: the 1906 USN sizing (four lights equal one generator) shows designers counted all the lights burning together as a real case. The 1894 doctrine shows not every light burned at once in a fleet; each lit only when needed, often only a minority. A night-action battle load could take the lights on the engaged side (half the ship's) plus margin. Treat it as intermittent: a light is "ready" (arc struck behind a shutter, which still draws full current) or "dark" (arc out).
- [EST] Arc behaviour on power loss: the arc goes out at once. Re-striking takes seconds once power is back. A light with hand training can still be pointed, but gives no light without current.

### Gaps
- Numbers carried per ship type by navy and period: only the 1894 summary was found. RN Dreadnought-era battleships typically had 6-10 × 24-inch, later 36-inch; German ships 6-8 × 110 cm (Bismarck 7 × 150 cm). These are unsourced recollections that need dreadnoughtproject or navweaps checking.
- The 36-inch, 60-inch, 110 cm and 150 cm naval lights' A/V figures. The dreadnoughtproject page did not load.

## Lighting: kW, lamp counts, emergency and secondary lighting, battle lanterns

### Takeaway
USN lighting around 1906 was 16 cp carbon-filament lamps at 3.5 W per candle-power, about 56 W each, on 80 V or 125 V circuits, plus enclosed arc lamps in each fire room and engine room. Tungsten lamps (1.0 W per cp) cut lighting load by about 70% from the 1910s. I found no warship lamp count. A merchant datum shows the scale: Titanic had 10,000 lamps with 500 on an emergency circuit fed by 2 × 30 kW sets.

### Cited Findings
- USN lamp ratings: 16 cp general lighting, 32 cp signals and running lights, 150 cp diving, 5/10/2/1/6 cp instruments, telephotos and torpedo. Allowance "four for each outlet" (spares). — [USNI 1906](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1906/october/electrical-installations-united-states-navy)
- Voltage: 80 V mean (78-82 V) on the older 80-volt dynamos; 123 V standard lamps on 125-volt installations. Efficiency standard 3.5 W per cp (3.3-3.7). Life about 400 h. Tungsten noted as coming, at about 1.0 W per cp, a 71% energy saving. The 150 cp lamp draws 6.25 A. — [USNI 1906](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1906/october/electrical-installations-united-states-navy)
- Arc lamps for lighting: enclosed arc rated "43½ A" (as extracted), adjustable for 80, 110 or 125 V. One for each fire room, one forward and one abaft each main engine. Open arcs at about 50 V, enclosed at about 85 V. — [USNI 1906](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1906/october/electrical-installations-united-states-navy) [UNCERTAIN: "43½ A" is likely an OCR error for 4½ A; enclosed arcs of the period drew about 5 A]
- Merchant comparison (Titanic, 1912): 4 × 400 kW sets at 100 V; 2 × 30 kW emergency sets; 10,000 incandescent lamps of 16-100 cp; 500 lamps on the emergency circuit; 153 motors and 562 electric heaters. — [British Wreck Commissioner's Inquiry report, electrical](https://www.titanicinquiry.org/BOTInq/BOTReport/botRepElec.php)

### Inferences
- [EST] Per-lamp load: 16 cp carbon ≈ 56 W; 32 cp ≈ 112 W; 16 cp tungsten ≈ 16-20 W. A heavy ship of about 1906 with 1,500-2,500 lamps, perhaps half burning, draws 40-70 kW on carbon lamps, or 15-25 kW on tungsten from about 1912. A cruiser is about half that; a destroyer about 100-200 lamps, or 3-10 kW.
- [EST] Failure behaviour for the game: main lighting dies with its switchboard or section. Secondary or emergency lighting (separate circuits, emergency dynamo or batteries) keeps a share, on the Titanic pattern of about 5%. Below that are hand lanterns: oil or candle lamps in RN and German WWI practice, and in USN WWII practice battery "battle lanterns" that light automatically on loss of power through a relay. Men in darkened spaces work slower; a turret or magazine without light largely stops.

### Gaps
- "Dreadnought 1,500 lamps" or any warship lamp count, and per-navy battle lanterns (oil, candle, battery) with dates. None was found in the sources reached.

## Ventilation: electric vs steam fans, magazine cooling, forced draught, what stopping the fans did

### Takeaway
Few hard figures were found. The 1918 USNI case shows a WWI USN battleship's machinery spaces depended on the ventilation system: motors overheated even with canvas chutes from it. Titanic's stokehold fans (12) and engine-room fans (6) were electric by 1912. Whether warship forced-draught fans were steam- or motor-driven by navy is not established from sources here.

### Cited Findings
- On a USN battleship in 1918, a 35 hp emergency motor in the machinery spaces had to be cooled by air "from the ship's ventilating system through canvas chutes", then by wet burlap on its casing. — [USNI 1918, "Emergency Repairs on a Battleship"](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1918/january/emergency-repairs-battleship)
- Tennessee (1919): fans supply air to each engine room to ventilate the propulsion generators. — [USNI 1919, "Electric Plant of the Battleship Tennessee"](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1919/august/electric-plant-battleship-tennessee)
- Titanic: 12 electrically driven stokehold fans and 6 for engine and turbine room ventilation. — [Titanic inquiry](https://www.titanicinquiry.org/BOTInq/BOTReport/botRepElec.php)

### Inferences
- [EST] For 1890-1925 coal-burning warships (RN, German, USN), forced-draught (closed-stokehold) fans were usually driven by small steam engines on the stokehold steam. Losing electric power did not stop them; losing steam did. Space ventilation fans became electric from the 1890s (USN first), with the RN still mixed. In WWII, USN forced-draught blowers were commonly turbine-driven. Verify all of this.
- [EST] Effect of stopping fans: in a stokehold under forced draught, losing the fans drops steam rate at once (boilers fall to natural draught, a large cut in output). Stopped space ventilation turns engine rooms and magazines hot within minutes to tens of minutes. Below decks smoke and fumes (cordite, CO from fires) are no longer cleared, and men are withdrawn. Model it as a heat or fume level rising at a rate per space.
- [EST] The WWII belief that ventilation was the single largest ship's service load is plausible: hundreds of fans, magazine cooling and air conditioning in USN ships. It is unverified here.

### Gaps
- Fan counts and kW per ship, magazine cooling plants (CO2 machines), and the speed of heat build-up were not found. The likely sources are Friedman, Brown's *Grand Fleet*, and Naval Machinery 1941.

## Pumps: drainage, bilge and fire; steam vs electric; capacities and kW

### Takeaway
One hard datum: a WWI USN battleship's steam-turbine main circulating pump (about 75 IHP) was replaced in an emergency by a 35 hp electric motor drawing 250 A at 120 V (about 30 kW), on a 125 V navy main. Tennessee's (1919) main circulating pumps were 235 hp DC motors on a 240 V bus. So big pumps are a kW-scale load once electrified: 30 kW to about 175 kW each.

### Cited Findings
- The original drive: a centrifugal circulating pump driven by an impulse turbine of about 75 IHP at 600 rpm. The emergency replacement: a 35 hp motor adapted from a deck winch, 400 rpm, 250 A at 120 V. "Navy power circuits carry 125 volts." — [USNI 1918](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1918/january/emergency-repairs-battleship)
- With one pump cross-connected to both condensers, speed was limited to about 10 kn. On motor failure, a signal warned the officer of the watch, who could stop the engines to save the condenser. Two generators were kept running and a watch was stood at all times. Power cut and restored with the rheostat not at "off" could short the armature and blow the fuses. — [USNI 1918](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1918/january/emergency-repairs-battleship)
- Tennessee: main circulating pumps on 235 hp DC variable-speed motors; condensate pumps vertical electric centrifugals; exciters off the ship's DC busses at a normal 240 V. — [USNI 1919](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1919/august/electric-plant-battleship-tennessee)

### Inferences
- [EST] 235 hp ≈ 175 kW of motor output, about 190 kW drawn, per pump. In turbo-electric ships the auxiliaries are a big electric load in their own right.
- [EST] Typical by navy: RN and German WWI drainage and fire pumps were mostly steam-driven reciprocating pumps (Worthington or Downton hand pumps as a backup), working while steam held. USN moved to electric centrifugals in the 1910s-20s. WWII ships of all navies had electric fire and drainage pumps with portable submersibles. I found no capacity or kW figures for these.

### Gaps
- Fire and bilge pump capacities, kW, German "Lenzpumpen", and WWII portable submersible pumps. None was found.

## Steering gear: steam vs electro-hydraulic, kW, control, hand fallback

### Takeaway
Only the late end was sourced. Massachusetts (BB-59, WWII) had electro-hydraulic gear: two pump units, either fed from either of two main switchboards by automatic bus transfer, putting the rudder 35°-35° in at most 36 s at 28 kn. USNI articles on electric steering exist from 1893 (Van Duzer-Mason) and 1909, but were not read.

### Cited Findings
- Massachusetts (BB-59): electric-hydraulic, two pump units, two pairs of rams. 35° right to 35° left in not more than 36 s at 28 kn ahead, 60 s at 14.5 kn astern. Each rudder about 273 sq ft. The standby unit is normally off but started "under critical conditions of navigation" for a quick change-over. Power from either of two main switchboards, "automatically controlled by the bus transfer equipment by means of voltage relays". — [NavSource, BB-57 class steering page](https://navsource.org/archives/01/57%20Excess%20pages/57z.htm)
- Titles only, for the writer: [USNI 1893, "Van Duzer-Mason Electric Steering Gear"](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1893/april/van-duzer-mason-electric-steering-gear) and [USNI 1909, "A Simple Electric Steering Gear"](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1909/january/simple-electric-steering-gear) show the USN trying electric steering from 1893.

### Inferences
- [EST] Pattern: RN and German WWI capital ships had steam steering engines (two or more, fore and aft), with telemotor (hydraulic) control from the bridge and hand steering wheels aft needing many men and far slower. Electro-hydraulic became standard in the 1920s-30s. Steering motor kW is likely about 50-150 kW per pump unit in a WWII battleship. Unverified.
- [EST] Failure ladder for the game: (1) the steering motor loses power, so bus transfer to the other switchboard takes seconds; (2) both lost, so start the standby or local control in the steering room; (3) hand steering at a fraction of the rudder rate, only at reduced speed. A steam steering engine instead fails with the steam line.

### Gaps
- Steering motor kW, hand steering men and rates, and RN/German steam engine details.

## Hoists, cranes, capstans, coaling winches, refrigeration, galley, laundry, distillers, workshops, wireless

### Takeaway
I found no figures for these. The 1918 article shows deck winches on a WWI USN battleship were electric, about 35 hp class. Titanic had electric cranes and winches with boat winches on the emergency circuit.

### Cited Findings
- The 35 hp circulating-pump motor was "adapted from a deck winch", so USN deck winches of about 1918 were electric at that size. — [USNI 1918](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1918/january/emergency-repairs-battleship)
- Titanic (merchant): electric cranes (six 50 cwt, two 30 cwt), four 3-ton cargo winches, four 15 cwt boat winches (on the emergency sets), and electric watertight doors released by magnets. — [Titanic inquiry](https://www.titanicinquiry.org/BOTInq/BOTReport/botRepElec.php)

### Inferences
- [EST] USN electrified hoists, turret training and winches early, around 1900 with the Kearsarge class. The RN kept hydraulics (turrets) and steam (capstans, coaling winches, boat hoists) longer. Distillers are steam (heat, not kW), as are the main refrigerating machines driven by steam in early ships. The galley went electric in the USN by the 1910s-20s. Each is a small to medium continuous load except hoists (intermittent, high during firing).

### Gaps
- All kW figures for this group.

## Ship-wide battle load: designers' breakdowns, USN load analysis practice, ventilation's share

### Takeaway
No sourced battle-load breakdown was found. The only design-sizing datum is the 1906 USN rule matching four searchlights to one 24 kW generator. The 1918 case shows two generators kept running at all times as a running reserve.

### Cited Findings
- Four 30-inch lights ≈ 23 kW ≈ one 24 kW generator (1906). — [USNI 1906](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1906/october/electrical-installations-united-states-navy)
- Two generators kept running and a watch at all times while an essential electric pump was on the line. — [USNI 1918](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1918/january/emergency-repairs-battleship)

### Inferences
- [EST] A rough battle load for a heavy ship of about 1910 on a mixed electric/steam plant: lighting 20-50 kW, searchlights 25-60 kW at night, ventilation 50-150 kW, hoists and training (USN) 100+ kW intermittent, pumps (electric only) 30-200 kW. All of it is to verify against Naval Machinery or Friedman.

### Gaps
- USN cruising/battle/anchor load analysis tables, RN practice, and Doerry & Amy 2019. Not reached.

## Turbo-electric propulsion as its own category

### Takeaway
In Tennessee (1919) the propulsion was 2 turbo-generators of 13,500 kVA (15,000 kVA max) feeding 4 × 7,000 hp motors. This is separate from the ship's service plant, which was a DC system at 240 V supplying the exciters and the big electric auxiliaries (235 hp circulating pumps). The 1919 article says the particulars of the other electric equipment are not available.

### Cited Findings
- 2 turbo-generators, 13,500 kVA full speed, 15,000 kVA max continuous, 2-pole, 3-phase, 36.5 Hz at 2,190 rpm. 4 main motors of 7,000 hp at 180 rpm (8,375 hp at 186.5 rpm). Exciters from the ship's DC busses at 240 V. Main circulating pumps 235 hp DC motors. One turbine out "does not materially affect the ship, except at high speeds, say above 17 or 18 knots". — [USNI 1919](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1919/august/electric-plant-battleship-tennessee)

### Inferences
- [EST] For the game: model propulsion as its own power path, never in the ship's service sum. The ship's service DC still feeds the propulsion's own excitation and pumps, so losing ship's service can stop a turbo-electric ship.

### Gaps
- The number and kW of Tennessee's ship's service generators, and Lexington's or New Mexico's.
