# B. Star shell and other naval illuminants, 1890-1925 (WWII as a later check)
Status: draft    Updated: 2026-10-29    Request: [REQUESTS.md "Night fighting, 1890-1925 first"](../../REQUESTS.md); an appendix of [night-fighting.md](../night-fighting.md)

Tags: **[SRC]** a cited text says it; **[INFERRED]** follows from cited facts; **[EST]** my estimate; **[UNCERTAIN]** thin or conflicting.
Units: 1 international candle (cp) ≈ 0.98 cd, so the conversion is taken as 1 cp ≈ 1 cd throughout.

**How the research went.** About 120 fetches and searches were made. The shared web-search budget ran out partway through, so the later work used only direct fetches of known pages. The richest finds came from the Internet Archive's search-inside endpoint (`/fulltext/inside.php`) on scanned books: the 1920 *Jutland Official Despatches*, Jellicoe's *Crisis of the Naval War* and Corbett's *Naval Operations* vol. III. The Dreadnought Project's "Star Shell" page could not be read (robots disallowed). No primary source was found that gives the light output or burn time of a WWI naval star shell.

**Other illuminants, for scale [SRC].**
- WWI parachute aeroplane flare: "not less than 350,000 candlepower", burning "at least seven minutes", released at about 4,000 ft [17].
- US aircraft flares in 1918: 300,000-400,000 cp, lighting a circle 1.5 miles across from 1,500-2,000 ft [24].
- WWI star shells in general (Luckiesh 1920): the parachute type burns "ordinarily less than a minute"; the non-parachute type gives "a brilliant light for about ten seconds". Muzzle velocities run "as high as 2700 feet per second", and "perhaps 1000 feet per second" remains when the star is ejected. Field and naval guns reach "about four miles" [20].
- Modern magnesium/sodium-nitrate flare composition: 36,800 cd·s per gram of candle; 222 kcd for 70 s from 450 g [25]. This is an upper bound on efficiency; WWI compositions were certainly worse [INFERRED].

## 2. Technique

**German practice, 1916 [SRC].** Star shell came first and the searchlight followed:
- "on each occasion German ships fired a white star shell which opened up like a parachute, before switching on their searchlights. Searchlights were already trained on the ship before being switched on." (Jutland Despatches, p. 281, para. 134 [1])
- "The enemy's fire and working of searchlights was extremely accurate, while their use of star shells rendered a surprise torpedo attack almost impossible." (p. 410 [1])
- German destroyers fired star shell "preparatory to torpedo attack" (p. 460 [1]).

In short, star shell was used to find and identify the target, and the searchlight then held it for the guns [INFERRED].

**Burst placement.**
- Height: Krupp gives 300 m as best (1913 [17]); the USN about 1,000 ft above the target (1922 [12]); Italy about 400 yd above the target (WWII [22]). A modern 100-350 m [23].
- Beyond the target: no source found that states the 1890-1925 rule of bursting the shell beyond the target to silhouette her.
- USN 1944 War Instructions, para. 820: "One or more low bursts short of the target will ruin the illumination for some vessels". Several ships firing star shell together need "a definite control plan … to prevent interference" [26]. Read together, this implies a burst over or beyond the target, never short [INFERRED].

**USN 1944 doctrine [SRC, 26].**
- Star shell is seen from farther away than a searchlight, and it lights a larger area.
- Drawbacks: illumination takes time to establish; it is hard to keep up on a fast-changing bearing; low cloud makes it "uncertain and sometimes impossible"; it is "not suitable for use at very short range"; it means "reduction of volume of effective fire especially in destroyer type".
- Searchlights are effective at only 4,000-6,000 yd.
- Combined use: open with searchlights and start star shell at the same time, then switch the searchlights off once star shell has the target lit.

**Keeping a target lit.** No WWI source gives the number of shells or the interval. A WWII later check, USS *Fletcher* at Ormoc Bay, December 1944 [27]:
- Opening: "3 - 5"/38 gun salvos"; "fire two salvos of stars".
- Guns 2, 3 and 4 were then shifted to keep the target lit, with 1 and 5 held for AA.
- 92 star shells went in about 7 minutes of firing (0151-0154 and 0204-0208), which is about 13 rounds a minute from three guns [INFERRED].
- The first firing lit nothing: "no target illuminated".

**Who fired it.**
- At Jutland: German light cruisers, destroyers and battle cruisers ("The leading Battle Cruiser then fired a star shell", p. 403 [1]).
- RN 1917-18: destroyer leaders and monitors [4], and a shore battery [2].
- WWII RN: the Battle class used its 4.5" "mainly for firing starshell" [28].

## 3. Cases in the record

**Port Arthur, 1904, on land.** Russian star shells worked with searchlights. They came "in rapid succession all over the field of operations"; the stars were "sinking slowly" and the light was "of great intensity" [SRC 18]. Naval use at Port Arthur was not found.

**Belgian coast, 2 Oct 1915.** Admiral Bacon's four monitors "steamed up the coast past Ostend under clouds of star shells from the enemy" [SRC 3, p. 185]. These came from the German coast defences [INFERRED].

**Jutland, 31 May-1 Jun 1916.** All of these are from the Official Despatches [1].
- Seen from about 20:45-21:30, at dusk:
  - "8.45 p.m. a star light was seen falling … which lit up the rear end of the Second Battle Squadron" (p. 80).
  - "9.14 Observed star shell on starboard bow" (pp. 78, 163, 456).
  - "9.17 Enemy fired one large star shell" (p. 397).
  - "9.25 Star shell fired" (p. 381).
  - "About 2130, a German star shell was fired on our starboard beam" (p. 484).
- Near *Chester*, about the same time: "enemy fired a star shell which lit up an area of about 1,000 yards" (p. 370).
- "A big flare was seen lasting quite 15 seconds" (8.55, p. 129). That this was a star shell is [UNCERTAIN].
- p. 403 (probably *Castor* and the 11th Flotilla [INFERRED]): "The leading Battle Cruiser then fired a star shell, which appeared to justify the opinion that they were enemy ships". The attack was not pressed because it was "not dark enough".
- Fleet narrative (p. 32): "a fair number of star shells were fired by the enemy, which gave out a brilliant illumination".
- About 23:50, a destroyer's report: "a Ship on our Starboard Beam fired one Gun. Several men say this was a Star Shell" (p. 429).
- *Fearless* (p. 173): a ship that "fired a star shell" before a heavy explosion. The same incident is on p. 327.
- Corbett: "the enemy star shells and the glare of the successive actions could be seen on the low clouds"; the flotillas were "overpowered by the enemy's searchlights, star-shells and secondary armament" [3, pp. 439, 443].
- **What the record does not give:** which German ships fired, or how many rounds. Southampton and the 2nd Light Cruiser Squadron and *Black Prince* were lit by searchlights in the accounts reached; star shell is not named in their cases [SRC 1, 29].

**Jellicoe's verdict, 1916:** "Their searchlights are superior to ours … their method of firing at night gives excellent results" (p. 13 [1]).

**Spring 1917, south coast of England.** German destroyer raids left unexploded German star shell ashore, and the Admiralty ordered it copied. The 6-inch guns at North Foreland got "the earliest supplies". When German destroyers next fired off North Foreland they "immediately withdrew, and never appeared again in 1917 in this neighbourhood" [SRC 2].

**Dover Strait, 14/15 Feb 1918.** British trawlers burning flares lit the barrage and were "sitting ducks"; flare-burning was "exceptionally vulnerable". No star shell is mentioned [SRC 4, 30].

**Off Dunkirk, 21 Mar 1918 (*Botha*).** British star shell in action:
- *Botha* "ordered star-shells to be fired to the north-east and north-west … but nothing could be seen".
- *Terror*'s "first star-shells, fired towards the Outer Ratel Bank, lit up three or four large destroyers".
- The German torpedo boats got under way "when they saw from the Botha's star shells that a division of British ships was approaching". Star shell gives the firer away [SRC 4].

**German U-boats, 1917.** Star shell was used as a signal: "Called up steamer by star shell"; "Stop steamer by white star-shell and Morse lamp signals" [SRC 31].

**Failures.** The only failures found:
- RN 1918 star shell that lit nothing [4].
- USN 1944 star shell that lit nothing [27].
- Star shell giving away the firer [4].

No figures on duds were found.

## 4. Other illuminants

- **Searchlights** remained the main illuminant to 1918; strand A covers them.
- **Very pistols** were used for signals: green for enemy destroyers, red and white for a submarine, in the Dover Patrol [SRC 4]. They are not illuminants at gun ranges [INFERRED].
- **Flares burned on trawlers** to light the Dover barrage, 1917-18 [SRC 4, 30].
- **Holmes lights** (calcium phosphide markers): not researched; nothing was found tying them to illumination at gun range.
- **Aircraft flares:** out of scope. The WWI figures are in §1.

Leads not read: Groos *Der Krieg in der Nordsee* vol. V, Campbell's *Jutland*, and German patents AT54315B, AT81557B and AT88175B ("Leuchtgeschoß"; the fetch failed).
- The number of star shell fired at Jutland, and by which ships.
- The calibres and performance of the RN's 1917 star shell.
- Japan and Austria-Hungary, 1904-1925.
- The French 75 mm army star shell (1915-16) figures.
- Russian naval star shell use at Port Arthur or in the Baltic.
- WWII candlepower of the USN Mk 30 and the RN 4.5" and 4" star shell.
- Fall rate, burst height and spacing in the period sources; and whether star shell was placed beyond the target before WWII.
