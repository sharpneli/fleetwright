# C1: Eyes in the dark (no radar): glasses, lookouts, first sightings, what showed, torpedo tracks, flash
Status: draft    Updated: 2026-10-29    Request: [REQUESTS.md "Night fighting, 1890-1925 first"](../../REQUESTS.md); an appendix of [night-fighting.md](../night-fighting.md)

Strand C1 of the night research. Era 1890-1925 first, WWII as a check. Tags: **[SRC]** sourced (the number is in a
fetched source), **[INFERRED]** worked out from sourced facts or standard physics, **[EST]** my estimate,
**[UNCERTAIN]** sourced but weak (secondary, forum, unclear conditions). Source numbers [n] refer to the list at the end.

The budget ran out partway through: the turn's web-search limit (200 searches, shared by all agents) was reached
after about 23 searches of mine. This report uses the material gathered up to then (about 45 fetches). See
"Not reached / open".

Context worked out for the Jutland night (31 May-1 June 1916) **[INFERRED]**:
- **Moon.** A mean-lunation count back from the new moon of 6 Jan 2000 (18:14 UT) puts a new moon at about
  1 June 1916, 03:00 UT (±~0.6 d). So the night was **moonless**.
- **Sun.** At about 56.5°N on 31 May (declination about +22°), the sun at local midnight is only about **11.5° below
  the horizon**, so the night never got past nautical twilight. By the same calculation:
  - about −10° at 22:15 GMT;
  - about −11.5° from 23:40 to 00:10;
  - about −6° at 02:00;
  - sunrise about 02:55 GMT.
- **Conclusion.** "Night" at Jutland was a moonless sky in deep nautical twilight, with mist (visibility at dawn was
  "three to four miles" [9]). It compares directly with the game's "sun 12° down" case.

**Transmission.** MgF₂ coating raises a 7×50's overall transmission "from 57.8% to 75%" [SRC] [8]. So an
**uncoated 7×50 transmits about 0.58** [SRC]. No navy-specific transmission figure for 1890-1925 glasses was found.
Pre-1935 glasses were all uncoated [INFERRED from [1]], so about 0.55-0.6 is fair for a Porro glass of that period
[INFERRED]. Roof and pentaprism designs (the Hensoldt Dialyt and pentaprism glasses) lose more [SRC: Cleary, for
pentaprisms] [3].

**Theory.**
- Cleary (USN, 1914) took the dark-adapted pupil as **0.2 in (5.1 mm)**. He held that a night telescope's exit pupil
  must be no smaller than that, that night magnification "cannot exceed 8 or 9 diameters", and that service needed a
  field of at least 4° [SRC] [3].
- Low power (3-4×) had been the received wisdom for night glasses before that [SRC] [3].
- Company Seven: a coated 7×50 gives "about 1.4 times better low light performance (18% better twilight performance)"
  than a 6×42 [SRC] [8].
- An exit pupil larger than the observer's own dark pupil wastes the excess. For a 5 mm eye, a 7×50 acts as a
  7×35 [INFERRED].
- For extended low-contrast targets at night, the gain is roughly in magnification × √(effective aperture). That
  favours the 10×45 and 12×60 Porro glasses over a 7×50 for a steady observer, and the 7×50 for one braced on a rolling
  ship [INFERRED].

**Were British glasses inferior at Jutland?** No document seen states it directly.
- The pieces that point that way:
  - The RN's popular glass was a 10×45 (4.5 mm exit pupil) [SRC] [1].
  - The KM had Zeiss 7×50 and 6×42 night-suited glasses from 1914 [SRC] [1].
  - Barr & Stroud's own 7×50 came only in 1932 [SRC] [4].
  - William Reed's book title (2001), "We're certainly not afraid of Zeiss", hints at the rivalry [SRC] [4].
- The secondary claim of German superiority in night fighting names "star shells and searchlights", not binoculars
  [SRC] [10].
- Treat "inferior British night glasses" as **[UNCERTAIN]**: plausible, but not documented here.

## Against the game's estimates

1. **The lookout's glass is a 7×50 with transmission 0.6 (uncoated).**
   - *Transmission confirmed*: an uncoated 7×50 transmits 57.8% [8].
   - *Glass revised by navy and year*:
     - KM: a 7×50 or 6×42 from 1914 is right [1].
     - RN 1914-18: use a 10×45 (4.5 mm exit pupil) or similar commercial glass [1]; a 7×50 only from 1932 (B&S CF30) [4].
     - USN pre-1918: an 8× instrument with a 2.5 mm exit pupil [3].
     - Pre-1905, any navy: Galilean or low-power glasses [2] **[INFERRED]**.
   - The 0.6 transmission holds for every uncoated Porro glass until 1935-43 **[INFERRED]**. Coated (WWII): **0.75**
     [8].
   - Let the eye's pupil cap the exit pupil: 5 mm was the 1914 USN design figure [3]. Older lookouts get less
     **[INFERRED]**.
2. **The rangefinder's exit pupil of 2.5 mm at 0.35 transmission: no evidence** found for rangefinders. A 2.5 mm exit
   pupil matches the USN's 8× Mark X telescope of 1914, which "would cut off 75% of the light" at night [3]. That
   makes the figure plausible for day-optimised instruments **[INFERRED]**.
3. **Sighting ranges.**
   - **Sun 12° down (destroyer 6.4 km, Lion 9.6 km): revise down for hazy northern seas.** Jutland's night was a
     moonless sky with the sun about 10-12° down [INFERRED], in mist. Cruisers and battleships there were first seen
     or challenged at **0.55-1.8 km** (600-2,000 yd; one "about 1 mile"; see §3). Ranges reached 2.7-4.6 km
     (3,000-5,000 yd) only at sun about −6° or after sunrise.
     - Even allowing that detection came before the challenge, the game's 9.6 km for a battlecruiser is **4-8× too
       long for Jutland's air**.
     - Either the game's haze model must cut these ranges (the daytime visibility was 4-8 miles), or the clear-air
       mean-sky figure is optimistic. Suggested anchor: big ship **about 1-2 km**, destroyer **about 0.6-1.2 km** at
       sun −10° to −12° with day visibility of about 6 km **[EST]**.
   - **Moonless clear (destroyer 2.0 km, Lion 3.5 km): plausible, no direct 1890-1925 evidence.**
     - The WWII Solomons give "often within 5,000 yd" (4.6 km) and 3,000 yd (2.7 km) between leading ships [30].
       Those were elite IJN lookouts with 12 cm glasses [6].
     - Savo's claimed 12,500-18,000 yd (11-16 km) [15] is weakly sourced and confounded by flares **[UNCERTAIN]**.
     - Keep the game's figures for ordinary 7×50 lookouts. An IJN-style lookout with big glasses might reach
       **about 1.5-2.5×** **[EST]**.
   - **Black overcast (destroyer 0.4 km, Lion 0.8 km): consistent.** Frost (1917) gives 700 yd (0.64 km) to the ship
     ahead in column on a dark night [27]. Point-blank meetings at Jutland (Broke at 150 yd [10]) fit too.
   - **Full moon 30° up (destroyer 9.8 km, Lion 13.6 km): no evidence** in this strand. By the
     moonless-to-full-moon illuminance ratio the figures are plausible. I'd flag them as long for side-lit or
     moon-backed geometry without data **[UNCERTAIN]**.
4. **Bow wave: height v²/2g × 0.3, length 0.15 of the ship, contrast 1.0: no numeric evidence.** The USN 1943 manual
   confirms bow wave, wash and wake as cues, chiefly through **phosphorescence** [11]. *Add a bioluminescence switch*:
   it can make the bow wave and wake self-luminous, visible even with no ambient light, as the 1904 torpedo tracks
   were [35]. Contrast 1.0 (white water against a dark sea) is reasonable in moonlight; in starlight alone it gives
   too little signal unless phosphorescent **[INFERRED]**.
5. **Funnel glow of 10 cd per funnel when forcing coal: no evidence for the number.** Confirmed only qualitatively:
   German torpedo boats were ordered to minimise sparks [10].
   - COLREG Annex I's light-range formula (not fetched; from memory) puts about 4.3 cd at 2 nm and about 12 cd at
     3 nm. So 10 cd would be visible at about **5 km** to a naked-eye lookout in clear air **[INFERRED]**.
   - Check that this is wanted. It would make a forcing coal-burner betray herself at about 5 km on a dark night,
     before her hull shows.
   - Sparks would be intermittent, not steady **[EST]**.
6. **A broadside flash seen 15-30 km on a dark night, a destroyer's gun 29 km moonless: no distance found.**
   - Sources confirm that flashes were seen when no ships could be ("in the mist no ships could be distinguished"
     [9]; Scheer [25]) and served as aim points [30],[33].
   - The figures are plausible as sky glow beyond the horizon **[INFERRED]**, but unsupported.
   - A destroyer's gun being seen as far as a broadside (29 km against 15-30 km) looks inconsistent: flash energy
     scales with the charge. A 4-in gun should be seen at a fraction of a 12-in broadside's range **[INFERRED]**.
7. **Own-salvo dazzle with τ = 1 s, nearly blind for a few seconds, back by about 8 s: probably too fast for targets
   near threshold.**
   - Qualitatively confirmed: layers "blinded by the flash of firing" and losing the target [18].
   - Lab flash-blindness data give **10-110 s** recovery to dim targets for brief bright flashes, rising with flash
     energy [37].
   - Suggest **two time constants**: a photopic one of about 1-2 s (bright or lit targets: searchlit, burning,
     star-shelled) and a scotopic one of about **15-30 s** for silhouettes near threshold **[EST]**.
   - Spotters off the flash line or shielded recover faster.
   - The IJN's salvo-only night fire [6] is a doctrinal answer to the same problem.
8. **Torpedo track seen at 2 km by day, about 1.1 km at sun −8°, about 170 m on a moonless night: partly
   contradicted.**
   - In a phosphorescent sea, tracks were "clearly seen" at night in 1904 [35], so 170 m is too short when
     bioluminescence is on. Suggest that the night range scales with a bioluminescence factor, of order 0.5-1 km when
     bright **[EST]**.
   - Add the track's lag: the torpedo runs ">100 yards ahead of the wake" [11].
   - Oxygen torpedoes should get a far fainter track [36].
   - No sourced number for the daylight 2 km.
