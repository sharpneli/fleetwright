# E2: The night of Jutland in numbers (31 May to 1 June 1916)
Status: draft    Updated: 2026-10-29    Request: [REQUESTS.md "Night fighting, 1890-1925 first"](../../REQUESTS.md); an appendix of [night-fighting.md](../night-fighting.md)

Tags: **[SRC]** a source states it (numbered list at the end), **[INFERRED]** my reasoning from sourced facts, **[EST]** my own computation or estimate, **[UNCERTAIN]** sources conflict, or the attribution is weak.

**Clocks.** British reports use GMT. German naval times (Groos, Scheer, Campbell's German columns, and the English Wikipedia German-ship articles, which say "CET, one hour ahead") run **GMT+1**. The German Wikipedia says the German times are "Kriegssommerzeit, GMT+2" [SRC 27]. Matching events fits +1 h [INFERRED]:
- Westfalen v. Tipperary: 00:30 German against 23:30 GMT.
- Pommern: 03:10 German against 02:10 GMT.

A few secondary pages slip, though:
- The Wikipedia Rostock article puts her torpedo hit at "1:30", which fits only if it uses GMT+2 [UNCERTAIN].
- Scheer's despatch, as translated in the Official Despatches, has Frauenlob "hit by a torpedo at 12.45 a.m." [SRC 2, p.717 of the scan]. That is about 1 h 10 min after the British 22:21–22:35 GMT even allowing +1 h [UNCERTAIN, not reconciled].

**Below, all times are GMT unless marked "German".**

**Page references.** The Official Despatches [SRC 2] and Fawcett & Hooper [SRC 7] were read through archive.org's full-text search (snippets). Their "p." numbers are scan leaves, not printed pages.

**Method.** The web-search budget ran out partway through. After that, everything came from direct fetches of known pages and full-text search inside the two books.

## 1. Light and weather

### 1.1 The sun (computed)
My computation uses NOAA/Meeus low-precision formulae. Expect about ±0.1° in altitude and ±2 min in event times [EST].

Event times at 56.5°N 6.0°E, about the 2nd LCS and 4th Flotilla area:

| Event | Time GMT | Sun |
|---|---|---|
| Sunset (−0.83°) | 20:12 | |
| End of civil twilight (−6°) | 21:12 | |
| Deepest | 23:33 | **−11.5°**, azimuth 360° (due north) |
| Civil twilight begins (−6°) | 01:56 | |
| Sunrise | 02:55 | |

So at 56.5°N there is **no astronomical night, and not even nautical night: it is nautical twilight all night** [EST].

The southern encounters were a little darker:
- At 56.0°N 6.5°E (Achates' 23:15 position was 55°48'N 6°23'E [SRC 4]), the sun dipped just below −12° from 23:23 to 23:40 GMT, minimum −12.0° [EST].
- At 55.5°N it was below −12° from 22:50 to 00:14 [EST].
- Northern stations (57°N 5.5°E) bottomed at −11.0° [EST].

**What this means for the brighter sky** [EST, INFERRED]:
- The glow under the sun sat in the **NW at about 22:00, due N at 23:30, and NNE to NE at 01:00–02:00**.
- The darkest part of the sky ran from S through E.

### 1.2 The moon
- **Effectively new moon**, so no moonlight. My computation puts conjunction in ecliptic longitude at about 19:30 GMT on 31 May (±1 h with this low-precision model), with illumination about 0.0% through the night [EST].
- It set about 20:38 GMT (azimuth about 321°) and rose about 02:39 (azimuth about 38°), invisible as a sliver against the twilight [EST].
- No source I reached mentions the moon. The German Wikipedia night section also has no moon statement [SRC 27].

### 1.3 Visibility and weather stated through the night
- **~21:00, Jellicoe.** "Darkness was now rapidly setting in, the mist was increasing" [SRC 2, narrative; SRC 6].
- **~22:15, Nottingham (2nd LCS).** A Stettin-class cruiser "not more than 2 miles away, silhouetted against the evening sky, and so was lit up to some extent while we remained in darkness" [SRC 7, p.241].
- **22:40 by Dublin's clock.** "Sighted enemy's vessels on Starboard Beam, it being quite dark and rather misty" [SRC 2, p.233].
- **~23:00–23:30, 4th Flotilla.**
  - A Tipperary survivor: "The night was dark and Sea fairly calm" [SRC 2, p.428].
  - An F&H narrator (probably Spitfire's) [INFERRED]: "Owing to the darkness and mist … her hull could not be seen more than three-quarters of a mile away" [SRC 7, p.258]. That is about 1,300 yd.
- **~01:45–02:10, 12th Flotilla.** "Too light for searchlights to be of much use" [SRC 1, quoting the attack; Brooks fn 45]. Brooks calls the mist and pre-dawn light a partial advantage to the attackers [SRC 1]. A secondary account: "sufficiently light to make the powerful searchlights ineffective and the swirling mist made the destroyers difficult to see" [SRC 11].
- **02:35–03:30.** Moresby: the enemy were hidden by "mist and smoke". Champion: enemy destroyers "disappeared in the mist" [SRC 5; SRC 6].
- **Daylight, 1 June.** "The weather was very misty at daylight, visibility being only three to four miles" [SRC 2, p.36, C-in-C narrative]. The Gazette despatch says "three to four miles" [SRC 9]. A Malaya personal account: "We could see no more than two miles" [SRC 14].
- **Wind and sea.**
  - Night: sea "fairly calm" [SRC 2, p.428].
  - By the forenoon of 1 June: "Wind, N.W., 6 to 7. Somewhat heavy sea" [SRC 2, p.41].
  - Secondary: "the weather was deteriorating with increasing mist and rising winds" towards dawn [SRC 11].
  - Broke and Sparrowhawk met heavy seas on 1–2 June [SRC 26; SRC 23].
- **Cloud.** No night cloud statement was reached [UNCERTAIN]. Daytime reports mention haze and patches ("misty in patches with varying visibility") [SRC 2, p.203].

### 1.4 Which side had the light
- The High Seas Fleet came from the **W to NW** of the British rear forces, steering SE to SSE. The British flotillas and the 2nd LCS steered S with the Germans on their **starboard beam or quarter**: Castor "starboard bow", Southampton "starboard beam", Tipperary "starboard beam" [SRC 2; SRC 4; SRC 7].
- So **British eyes looked W to NW and later N, towards the brighter twilight**, and **German eyes looked E to SE into the dark sky** [INFERRED from geometry and §1.1].
- This is the reverse of the hypothesis in the brief that the British looked south against the dark. It holds at least for the first-contact geometry of the 11th Flotilla, 2nd LCS and 4th Flotilla actions.
- Direct statements:
  - Nottingham, above: the German silhouetted against the evening sky, "while we remained in darkness" [SRC 7, p.241].
  - Petard, after midnight, ship on the starboard bow about 600 yd: "we could see clearly large crane derricks … against the sky, and only German ships have these fittings" [SRC 7, p.295].
  - In daylight, earlier, the evening silhouette effect is described: German ships "against the light of the sunset whilst our background was a mass of dark cloud" [SRC 7, p.172].
- The Germans' counter to this was lights. They opened with searchlights, so the British side being darker bought nothing once a light was switched on [INFERRED].
- In the Spitfire account, the searchlights put Tipperary's hull "silhouetted against the darkness, getting clearer and clearer as each successive searchlight focussed itself upon her" [SRC 7, p.258].

### 2.2 Southampton, the numbers that conflict
- **Opponents.** Goodenough: "five enemy ships, apparently a cruiser and four light cruisers" [SRC 3]. A Southampton narrator: "a line of five ships" [SRC 7, p.243]. The record's "four cruisers" are four ships firing on Southampton and one on Dublin [SRC 12, from Bennett].
- **Range.**

  | Range | Source |
  |---|---|
  | "scarcely 1,500 yards" at sighting | [SRC 7, p.243] |
  | 1,500 yd at sighting | [SRC 12, Bennett] |
  | Nottingham: "little over 2,000 yards" | [SRC 7, p.242] |
  | torpedo fired at 1,500–2,000 yd | [SRC 1] |
  | 730 m (~800 yd) | German-side figure [SRC 20] |
  | 800 yd | secondary [SRC 11] |

  **Best band: 800–2,000 yd, about 1,500 yd typical** [INFERRED].
- **Duration.** "about 15 minutes" (the whole action) [SRC 3]. The narrator: "lasted no more than four minutes, though it seemed very much longer" [SRC 7, p.245]. An F&H snippet reads: "After two minutes of action the Southampton was badly on fire abreast her funnels" [SRC 7].
- **Casualties, three counts.**
  1. Signal of 1 June: "28 men killed, 35 men seriously injured, one officer and five men less seriously" = 69 [SRC 2, p.618].
  2. The narrator: "35 men killed or died of wounds, and 1 officer and 54 men wounded" = **90**. Her "broadside had been reduced from five 6-inch guns to two" [SRC 7, p.245]. Southampton is inferred from the five-gun 6 in broadside and the page [INFERRED].
  3. Of three guns' crews: "11 killed and 16 wounded, 27 out of a total of 33". Of the searchlight crews: "6 killed and 14 wounded out of a similar total of 33" [SRC 7, p.245].
- **Hits.** Southampton took 18 hits [SRC 13 (navalgazing)] [UNCERTAIN]. She had three fires, "quickly put out" [SRC 3].

## 5. What the British lacked and the Germans had
- **Jellicoe, letter of 18 June 1916**: "The German organisation at night is very good. Their system of recognition signals is excellent. Ours is practically nil. Their searchlights are superior to ours and they use them with great effect. Finally, their method of firing at night gives excellent results … under night conditions we have a good deal to learn from them" [SRC 2, p.13].
- **Jellicoe, *The Grand Fleet* (1919)**:
  - "the use of star shell, at that time unfamiliar to us, was of the greatest use to them in locating our destroyers" [SRC 8, p.453].
  - German searchlights "were not only very powerful (much more so than ours)" and their method of "bringing guns and searchlights rapidly on to any vessel sighted was excellent" [SRC 8, p.453].
  - "Star shells were fired with great frequency by the enemy, and they produced a very brilliant illumination" [SRC 8, p.454].
  - "The greater efficiency of German searchlights at the time of the Jutland action" was a reason not to seek a night battle-fleet action [SRC 8, p.445].
- **Recognition.**
  - The Germans used coloured-lamp groups that the British could not copy. The British reports record:
    - "two red over one white" [SRC 5; SRC 7, p.295];
    - two red over one green [SRC 5];
    - "four red lights horizontal, and four green horizontal" (Moltke) [SRC 2, p.148];
    - "a vertical string of coloured lights, some green and some red" [SRC 7, p.259];
    - "coloured lights at their fore yardarms" [SRC 7, p.249].
  - The British flashed a lettered challenge by lamp [SRC 7; SRC 4]. The Germans had read part of it. Castor was challenged with "the first two signs of the challenge of the day" [SRC 4].
  - The Commodore (F) ties this to a ship asking by searchlight for the day's challenge and reply. In the record of messages: "Please give me challenge and reply now in force as they have been lost. (Challenge and reply passed as requested.)" [SRC 2, p.593; SRC 4]. This was Lion asking Princess Royal at about 22:00 [SRC 13 (navalgazing)] [UNCERTAIN on the time].
  - Every British challenge made at short range was answered by searchlights and fire (Tipperary, Broke). Three captains held back from challenging or from firing for fear of giving away their division (Agincourt, Thunderer) or of firing on friends (11th Flotilla, Achates) [SRC 2; SRC 4; SRC 7].
- **Searchlights.**
  - German lights had iris shutters for rapid on and off [SRC 12] [UNCERTAIN, secondary].
  - Their lights were "already trained on the ship before being switched on" [SRC 2, p.281].
  - "German ships fired a white star shell" that "opened up like a parachute" before switching on searchlights [SRC 2, p.281].
  - Ambuscade: "The enemy's fire and working of searchlights was extremely accurate" [SRC 2, p.410].
  - British light-cruiser practice: ships that showed lights drew all the fire, and those that didn't were left alone [SRC 7, p.242, p.249].
  - British arc lights were slow to warm up, and the carbons glowed after switching off [SRC 13 (navalgazing)] [UNCERTAIN, secondary].
- **Star shell.** The Germans fired it from 8.8 cm guns: Thüringen at Turbulent [SRC 19]; "lit up an area of about 1,000 yards" [SRC 2, p.370]. The British had none in service [SRC 8, "unfamiliar to us"].
- **Torpedo-boat design.** The 4th Flotilla's Acasta-class boats "had only two torpedo tubes" [SRC 1].
- **Post-Jutland reforms.** The Grand Fleet night-fighting committee, the adoption of British star shell (generally given as 1917), and searchlight directors and control were **not reached** with sources in this pass. Jellicoe's 1919 book and the 1916 letter are the participant statements found [SRC 2; SRC 8].

## 6. Patterns
1. **Range grows with the sun.** Contact and firing ranges follow the sun's depression closely [INFERRED, using §1.1 and §2]:

   | Time GMT | Sun | Sighting or firing range | Source |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | ~22:05 (Castor) | −9° | lights at 2,500 yd, fire at 2,000 yd | |
   | ~22:15 (2nd LCS) | −10° | sighted at 1,500–2 miles | |
   | 23:15–00:30 (4th Flotilla, Black Prince, Petard) | −11.5 to −12° | 150–2,000 yd, typically 600–1,500 yd; hulls invisible beyond about 1,300 yd | |
   | ~01:45–02:10 (12th Flotilla) | −8 to −5.5° | 1,700–3,500 yd; too light for searchlights | |
   | 02:35 (Moresby) | −3° | 4,000 yd | |
   | dawn | – | 3–4 miles in mist | |

   A model keyed to the sun's depression, plus a mist term, would reproduce this.
2. **Who saw first.** The British usually saw first, because the Germans were against the brighter NW to N sky. Hesitation over identity then gave the Germans the first effective blow. The Germans "usually got the first shot in" [SRC 13 (worldwar1)]. Their sequence: recognition lights or a challenge, then searchlights already trained, then rapid secondary fire within seconds [SRC 2; SRC 7].
3. **Lights draw fire.** A ship that showed lights or fired was the one hit. Dark ships in the same line went untouched (Nottingham and Birmingham) [SRC 7].
4. **Short and decisive.** Fights lasted seconds to about 5 minutes per contact; 15 minutes at the outside (2nd LCS) [SRC 3; SRC 7; SRC 15].
5. **Hit rates.**
   - Secondary-gun hit rates at 1,000–2,000 yd under searchlights were high. Tipperary, Fortune, Ardent and Black Prince were wrecked within minutes [SRC 15; SRC 13].
   - Destroyer torpedoes at 800–1,000 yd and forward inclinations hit about once in 14 (4th Flotilla) [SRC 1].
   - The 12th Flotilla's attack from abaft the beam at 1,700–3,500 yd, in pre-dawn light and mist, got 1 in 17 [SRC 1].
6. **Collisions and rams were common.** Four in one flotilla action: Spitfire with Nassau, Broke with Sparrowhawk, Contest with Sparrowhawk, Posen with Elbing [SRC 1]. Two of the eleven night losses were by collision: Sparrowhawk and Elbing [INFERRED].
