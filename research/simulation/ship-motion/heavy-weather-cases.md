# Heavy weather, sea state, ship motion and gunnery: historical cases 1890–1990
Status: draft    Updated: 2026-10-12    Request: -    Appendix to: [ship-motion-gunnery-research.md](ship-motion-gunnery-research.md)

Scope note: these notes cover 21 web fetches and searches. Primary despatches were reachable for Coronel (von Spee and Luce reports, BYU WWI archive) and North Cape (Fraser despatch, London Gazette 38038). Most other cases rest on secondary sources: Wikipedia, navweaps, navalgazing, kbismarck, and Castles of Steel (Massie, via erenow). Tags:

- **[I]** means my inference.
- **[?]** means the hit count is uncertain or the sources conflict.

## Q1. Case table: actions where weather or motion affected gunnery

### Takeaway
The best-documented weather-to-gunnery mechanisms fall into four groups:
- **Low casemates closed or awash.** Seen at Coronel in 1914.
- **Forward turrets and optics swamped when steaming into a head sea at speed.** Seen at Lofoten in 1940, which forced Renown down to 20 kn and flooded the German forward turrets.
- **Spray on the big turret rangefinders.** Seen at Denmark Strait in 1941.
- **Destroyers unable to close, keep speed or fight in force 7–8.** Seen at North Cape in 1943.

In every one of these cases the side that steered with the sea on the bow or quarter, keeping its weapons dry, out-shot the side heading into it. The numerical hit rates are almost never broken out by motion alone.

### Cited Findings

**Coronel (1 Nov 1914)**
- Von Spee's report says the wind and swell were "head on and the vessels had heavy going", that "the seas … washed over the bridges", and that "The swell was so great that it obscured the aim of the gunners at the six inch guns on the middle deck." He adds, "I assume that the heavy sea made more trouble for them than it did for us." [S] — [Spee report, BYU WWI archive](https://net.lib.byu.edu/~rdh7/wwi/1914/spee.html)
- Luce (HMS Glasgow) reports: "Growing darkness and heavy spray of head sea made firing difficult, particularly for main deck guns." Monmouth "turned away to get stern to sea." The enemy opened fire at 12,000 yd. [S] — [Luce report, BYU](https://net.lib.byu.edu/~rdh7/wwi/1914/spee.html)
- Massie writes that "The main deck casemates of Good Hope and Monmouth had to be kept closed lest the guns be smothered by the sea." He also writes that Glasgow "never observed any gunfire at all from the lower gun casements," and that Glasgow's layers were "firing from a rolling platform only eight feet above the waterline". Spee was "steaming at twenty knots against a heavy sea", with spray soaking "the men in the forward turret". [S] — [Massie, Castles of Steel ch.13 (erenow)](https://erenow.org/ww/castles-of-steel/13.php)
- Scharnhorst fired 3 salvos/min against the British one. Scharnhorst fired 422 rounds of 8.2-in and Gneisenau 244. Scharnhorst made ≥35 hits on Good Hope. The German light cruisers fired more than 600 shells at Glasgow and hit her 5 times. Scharnhorst was hit twice and Gneisenau 4 times. [S] — [Massie](https://erenow.org/ww/castles-of-steel/13.php); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Coronel)
- Wikipedia says the casemates "continually flooded if the gun doors were opened to fire in heavy seas." [S] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Coronel)
- [I] Roughly half of each British armoured cruiser's 6-in battery was effectively lost, along with the engaged side's lower casemates. Light and the gun-count advantage are confounded with motion here.
- [I] For the German armoured cruisers, ≥35 hits from 422 rounds gives a floor of about 8% for Scharnhorst while rolling heavily. Gneisenau's hits on Monmouth are unknown.
- [I] The light cruisers fired more than 600 rounds for 5 hits, under 1%, at long range in a heavy sea against a small target.

**Falklands (8 Dec 1914)**
- Wikipedia: "the sea was placid with a gentle breeze". It says the freshening NW breeze "caused the funnel smoke of the British ships to obscure their target practically throughout the action." The British made 25.5 kn and the Germans 22.5 kn. [S] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Falkland_Islands)
- [I] This is a control case: calm sea, high speed, with the losses coming from smoke rather than motion.

**Dogger Bank (24 Jan 1915)**
- From eyewitness letters: an Arethusa gunner says the sea burst into his gun's shield twice. A Sandfly officer says "our bridge was washing down" and "one could not keep binoculars dry." [S] — [BYU WWI archive, Dogger Bank](https://net.lib.byu.edu/~rdh7/wwi/1915/dogger.html)

**Heligoland Bight (28 Aug 1914)**
- Fog and mist. Cöln fired about 200 rounds at Lion for 5 hits. Lion hit Ariadne from her third salvo at about 3 miles. [S] — [Naval Gazing](https://www.navalgazing.net/Heligoland-Bight)

**Jutland (31 May 1916)**
- The sources describe haze and smoke and the British being to windward of their own smoke. No surface sea state is given. [S] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Jutland)
- **Gap:** I found no source for the destroyer weather at Jutland.

**Lofoten (9 Apr 1940)**
- Naval Gazing says Renown "was soon forced to reduce speed to 20 kts to keep her forward turrets in action at all". It describes "massive amounts of water over her bow", water "washing out the fire-control optics", and water "leaking into the turrets through the shredded blast bags". [S] — [Naval Gazing, Norway Pt 8](https://www.navalgazing.net/Norway-Part-8)
- It adds that the Germans turned north "straight into the seas", and that "Scharnhorst suffered flooding in both of her forward turrets through the ejection ports for the powder casings." [S] — [Naval Gazing](https://www.navalgazing.net/Norway-Part-8)
- According to the same source, Gneisenau's A turret was flooded through a gap left by a destroyed rangefinder cover, which "soon put it out of action". [S] — [Naval Gazing](https://www.navalgazing.net/Norway-Part-8)
- [?] Wikipedia's Scharnhorst article says Scharnhorst's Anton turret was "put out of action by severe flooding". It adds that 1939 trials had shown "a dangerous tendency to ship considerable amounts of water", which led to the Atlantic bow. Treat the reports of both ships' A turrets flooding as consistent. [S] — [Wikipedia Scharnhorst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_battleship_Scharnhorst)
- Wikipedia's Lofoten article: "waves breaking over Renown's forward turrets" forced a 20-minute break at about 05:00. Renown's starboard bulge, damaged by the sea and her own gunfire, limited her speed. [S] — [Wikipedia Lofoten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_off_Lofoten)
- Rounds fired: Renown 230 rounds of 15-in and 1,065 rounds of 4.5-in. Scharnhorst 182 rounds of 11-in and Gneisenau 54. [S] — [Wikipedia Lofoten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_off_Lofoten)
- [?] Hit counts on Gneisenau conflict:
  - The Renown article gives one 15-in and two 4.5-in. [S] — [Wikipedia Renown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Renown_(1916))
  - The Lofoten article says two shells and then a third. [S] — [Wikipedia Lofoten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_off_Lofoten)
- Gneisenau made 2 hits on Renown, both duds. [S] — same
- [I] Main-calibre hit rates in a storm at long range: Renown 1/230 ≈ 0.4%, Gneisenau 2/54 ≈ 3.7%, Scharnhorst 0/182 (her radar was failing).

**Denmark Strait (24 May 1941)**
- Spray drenched the lenses of Prince of Wales's A-turret 42 ft rangefinder and of both British ships' 30 ft B-turret rangefinders. They used the 15 ft director rangefinders instead. The British were steaming into the wind, and the Germans held the weather gauge. "rough seas" kept the destroyers out of it. [S] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Denmark_Strait)
- Rounds and hits: Bismarck fired 93 AP. Prinz Eugen fired 157. PoW scored 3 hits on Bismarck. Bismarck and Prinz Eugen hit PoW 7 times. Hood scored 0. [S] — same
- [I] Losing the long-base rangefinders roughly triples range error relative to the base length (42 ft against 15 ft). That is a useful model lever for "spray on forward rangefinders".

**Bismarck's final action (27 May 1941)**
- Wind NW "force 8 (34-40 knots)", "The sea ran high". Bismarck was "steering against the wind at seven knots." Rodney opened fire at 08:47 at about 20,000 m. In the final phase she was at 2,500–4,000 m. In total, 2,876 shells were fired at Bismarck. [S] — [kbismarck](https://kbismarck.com/bismarck-last-battle.html)
- Wikipedia notes "a gale-force storm complicated gunnery" and "unpredictable motions" made Bismarck an unstable gun platform. Tovey judged an attack from windward undesirable. [S] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_battle_of_the_battleship_Bismarck)
- From navweaps tech-016:
  - Rodney fired 375 rounds of 16-in and KGV 339 rounds of 14-in.
  - Each scored about 40 heavy hits.
  - Optical ranging was "extremely difficult" due to long range and funnel haze.
  - KGV's A turret was out for 30 min with a jammed shell. Five of her ten guns jammed at times.
  - [S] — [navweaps tech-016](https://navweaps.com/index_tech/tech-016.php)
- [?] The navweaps 16-in page gives Rodney "380 rounds". It also says Nelson-class "Rate of Train was limited to 2 degrees per second when the ship's roll exceeded 5 degrees", and that turret damage came from lateral thrust "especially when in a seaway". [S] — [navweaps 16"/45 Mk I](https://navweaps.com/Weapons/WNBR_16-45_mk1.php)
- [I] At about 40/375 ≈ 11% and 40/339 ≈ 12% in a force 8 sea, the British shooting confirms that a steady firing ship against a slow, near-stationary target still hits well. Target motion mattered less than own-ship steadiness and closing the range.

**North Cape (26 Dec 1943): Fraser despatch, London Gazette 38038**
- "There was an unpleasant sea and conditions in DUKE OF YORK were most uncomfortable" (¶16). [S] — [Gazette 38038](https://thegazette.co.uk/London/issue/38038/data.pdf)
- "my destroyers had much difficulty in avoiding broaching to" and "the DUKE OF YORK'S bows were constantly under water" (¶21, following sea). [S] — same
- Burnett's cruisers came from the south to avoid "steaming into the strong south westerly wind and heavy seas" (¶22). [S] — same
- "with wind force 7-8 from the south west, Force I's maximum speed was 24 knots". The weather gave the enemy "an advantage of 4-6 knots in speed" (¶28, ¶32). [S] — same
- The destroyers' speed was reduced. The 36th Division "owing to the heavy sea were unable to close to attack" (¶37, ¶42). [S] — same
- A heavy sea struck Matchless's bridge and broke communications to her torpedo tubes (¶73). [S] — [kbismarck summary of despatch](https://kbismarck.com/scharnhorst-sinking.html)
- DoY opened fire at 12,000 yd. She was "frequently straddled and there were many near misses". Her hits came "probably" on the 1st and 3rd salvos, with "at least three" hits in phase 1. Prisoners accounted for "at least ten" hits later. She checked fire at 21,400 yd. DoY was not hit, though both masts were holed by 11-in shells that did not explode (¶52–59, 68, 81–82). [S] — [Gazette 38038](https://thegazette.co.uk/London/issue/38038/data.pdf)
- [?] Duke of York hit totals vary by source:
  - Wikipedia's DoY article: 52 broadsides and "Ten 14-inch shells". [S] — [Wikipedia DoY](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Duke_of_York_(17))
  - Wikipedia's Scharnhorst article: "52 salvos … at least 13 hits". [S] — [Wikipedia Scharnhorst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_battleship_Scharnhorst)
  - Wikipedia's North Cape article: 31 of 52 salvoes straddled. [S] — [Wikipedia North Cape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_North_Cape)
- Scharnhorst's forward Seetakt radar was lost early. [S] — [Wikipedia North Cape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_North_Cape)
- **Gap:** I found no source saying DoY's A turret flooded from the sea. The despatch says a *hit* "may have put 'A' turret out of action", referring to Scharnhorst's A turret (¶57). [S] — [Gazette](https://thegazette.co.uk/London/issue/38038/data.pdf)
- [I] Applying 52 broadsides of 10 guns ≈ ≤520 rounds against 13 hits gives roughly 2.5–3% for radar-controlled 14-in in force 7–8 at night. This uses the "at least 13" figure.

**Barents Sea (31 Dec 1942)**
- Severe gales hit on 28–29 Dec. The action was fought in polar night, and Lützow's fire was "ineffective" because of smoke. No round counts were found. [S] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Barents_Sea)

**Komandorski (26 Mar 1943)**
- "The morning was cold and calm … Not a cloud in the sky." [S] — [HistoryNet](https://historynet.com/battle-of-the-komandorski-islands-close-call-in-the-aleutians-during-world-war-ii/)
- Salt Lake City fired 806 AP and 26 HC rounds. She was hit 5 times by Maya. [S] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Komandorski_Islands)
- [I] The premise that cold and spray degraded gunnery here is **not supported**. Komandorski is a calm-sea, long-range control case, and its low hit rate comes from range and evasion.

**Calabria (9 Jul 1940)**
- Warspite hit Giulio Cesare at "well over" 24,000 m. The Italian salvos were "too widely dispersed due to technical reasons." No weather data was found. [S] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Calabria)

**Samar (25 Oct 1944)**
- Sprague "headed for a nearby rain squall … Under cover of the rain". Visibility was poor for Yamato. [S] — [HistoryOfWar](https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/battles_samar.html)
- Following wind made the carrier smoke less effective. [S] — [NHHC](https://history.navy.mil/browse-by-topic/wars-conflicts-and-operations/world-war-ii/1944/samar.html)

### Inferences
- Heading relative to the sea is the controlling variable, more than sea state alone. At Lofoten the Germans deliberately ran into the sea and lost their forward turrets, while Renown had to slow to 20 kn. At North Cape the British avoided the head sea, and Fraser explicitly accepts losing 4–6 kn rather than steam into it. [I]
- Suggested model rule: when steaming into a head sea above about force 6 at more than about 20 kn, apply three penalties [I]:
  1. Forward turrets risk being disabled (flooding).
  2. Forward long-base rangefinders fall back to short-base.
  3. Casemates lower than about 3–4 m above the waterline cannot fire on the weather side, or on either side in very heavy seas.
- The Coronel casemates were described at about "eight feet above the waterline" for Glasgow's layers. Massie's figure is for Glasgow, not the armoured cruisers' main-deck casemates. [S/I]
- Light craft are hit hardest. Destroyers lose speed and torpedo opportunity rather than gunnery accuracy, as in Fraser ¶37 and ¶42. [I]

### Gaps
- I found no measured roll angles or pitch amplitudes for any action.
- I found no per-salvo breakdown linking hits to roll phase.
- Dogger Bank, Falklands, Java Sea, Matapan, Spartivento, Narvik, Kolombangara and typhoon actions were not reached for weather detail within the tool budget.
- The Jutland destroyer weather was not found.

## Q2. Trials: director firing, sea state and stabilisation

### Takeaway
The 1912 Thunderer–Orion trial is the canonical evidence for director firing in a seaway, but the public sources found here give only qualitative detail plus the repeat result. Stabiliser evidence before 1950 is weak: the Henderson gyro installation was judged a failure, and no hit-rate-vs-roll trial data was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Thunderer–Orion trial.** Thunderer "decisively outshot" Orion on 13 Nov 1912. Part of her advantage was her director being "above the smoke that obscured the target from Orion's guns." In a repeat "in better conditions on 4 December", Orion "performed much better, apparently beating Thunderer." [S] — [Wikipedia HMS Thunderer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Thunderer_(1911))
- [I] The repeat matters for calibration. The director's advantage largely disappears in good conditions, which supports making director-versus-local benefit a function of sea state, smoke and light.
- **USS Henderson gyro stabiliser.** It was designed for a 13 s roll period and to quench 3° per oscillation. A 1918 fire-control test suggested benefit, but the installation "can frankly be called a failure", with realistic quenching of about 1.5°. The author says an unstabilised ship rolls "fifteen degrees to twenty degrees to a side" and that steadiness "may decrease the dispersion", but that this is unproven. No hit rates are given. [S] — [USNI Proceedings, Sept 1923](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1923/september/ship-stabilization)
- A second account of Henderson says roll was cut to "3 degrees in the roughest seas". Denny-Brown fins were fitted to British warships from the late 1930s, with more than 100 installations by 1950. The USN kept stabilisers experimental until the 1950s, partly because better directors reduced the need. [S] — [Wikipedia Stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stabilizer_(ship))
- **USN WWII stabilised fire control.** The Mark 8 rangekeeper with a stable vertical gave "fully stabilized guns for firing in most weather conditions or sea states". IJN gyros "were neither accurate nor reliable." [S] — [navweaps tech-086](http://www.navweaps.com/index_tech/tech-086.php)

### Inferences
- Model director, stable-vertical and continuous-aim fire as reducing the roll penalty, not removing it. Model local and casemate laying as fully exposed to it. [I]

### Gaps
- I found no public source for 1912 trial sea state, roll angle or range (the project already holds 4/27 against 26/39).
- No USN practice-cancellation data was found.
- No post-war (1950–1990) stabiliser gunnery trial results were found (for example Black Swan or Daring fin stabilisers).

## Q3. Doctrine and design: sea-state limits, casemate height, "steady platform" claims

### Takeaway
I found no explicit "maximum sea state for main or secondary battery" doctrine. The design record shows that low casemates were treated as unusable in rough water. US pre-dreadnought bow casemates were abandoned as "untenable", and Iron Duke's 6-in battery flooded. This drove secondaries up into the superstructure and turrets.

### Cited Findings
- Great White Fleet experience: "Casemates for the bow 3-inch guns in the newer pre-dreadnoughts were untenable due to wetness". "Secondary armament was useless at speed" and was moved "much higher in the hull". The ships were "wet in all but the calmest seas". In "smooth to moderate seas" the bottom of the belt was visible. This led to Wyoming and Nevada design changes. [S] — [liquisearch / Wikipedia Great White Fleet](https://www.liquisearch.com/great_white_fleet/effects_on_us_capital_ship_design)
- Iron Duke class: the 6-in guns were "mounted too low in the hull, which subjected them to heavier pounding from rough seas". The port plates "were easily washed away … water easily entered the ship". This was fixed with dwarf bulkheads and rubber seals. [S] — [Wikipedia Iron Duke class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Duke-class_battleship)
- "As with all casemates, these proved very wet." [S] — [Naval Gazing, Secondary Armament Pt 1](https://www.navalgazing.net/Secondary-Armament-Part-1)
- Scharnhorst-class 1939 trials showed "a dangerous tendency to ship considerable amounts of water in heavy seas". Flooding damaged Anton turret electrics, and the ships were refitted with an Atlantic bow. [S] — [Wikipedia Scharnhorst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_battleship_Scharnhorst)
- Nelson class: train rate limited to 2°/s when roll exceeded 5°. [S] — [navweaps](https://navweaps.com/Weapons/WNBR_16-45_mk1.php)

### Inferences
- Candidate casemate rule [I]: guns with sills below about 3 m above the waterline (Coronel-era main-deck casemates; Glasgow's layers at about 8 ft) cannot fire above roughly sea state 5, or above state 4 into a head sea. Guns at about 4–5 m (Iron Duke) become intermittent. Superstructure and turret secondaries are largely unaffected until spray hits the optics.

### Gaps
- I found no sourced "steady gun platform" claims for Yamato, Iowa, Hood or Nelson, and no German "steady ship" claims, in the pages reached.
- I found no explicit navy doctrine stating a numeric sea-state firing limit.
- I found no casemate sill heights in metres for Monmouth or Drake class.
