# Striking colours, abandoning and scuttling: the decisions, 1911-1925

Tags: [SRC] a figure stated by the cited source; [INFERRED] worked out from cited figures; [EST] an estimate; [UNCERTAIN] sources conflict or the claim is weak. Times are local or as the source gives them. "WP" = English Wikipedia ship or battle page (each cites Campbell, Staff, Hildebrand/Röhr/Steinmetz, Corbett/Newbolt and so on). Coverage note: most rows rest on WP pages fetched in this pass, plus Glossop's despatch. The German official history, Campbell's *Jutland* and the Jutland despatches were not opened, so the German orders and several British destroyer figures are gaps (see each Gaps section).

## Q1. The case table: what each ship had left, who was near, who decided, what, and how long it took

### Takeaway
In 1911-1918, German and British ships that could no longer fight almost never struck. They fought their last gun, then abandoned ship or scuttled with the colours still flying. The decision came either when the last gun or the last ammunition was gone (minutes after the critical damage), or when flooding beat the pumps or an enemy came into sight while a friend stood by (hours after it). The real strikes were the beaten minor navies (the Ottoman Avnillah in 1912), Nürnberg once she was stopped and still under fire, Emden hours after she was beached and fired on again, the tiny Kingani after her captain was killed, and Dresden's flag of truce while her charges were set. Scuttling by one's own side, to keep a ship out of enemy hands, was the Russian and late-war pattern: Slava in 1917, Novorossiysk in 1918.

### Inferences
- **Two clocks run the decision.** (a) **The fighting clock.** The decision follows the last working gun or the last round within about 5-50 minutes: Königsberg 47 min after one gun was left; Emden about 20 min; Mainz about 20-35 min from the jam; Leipzig within minutes of running dry; Gneisenau's last shot 47 min before she sank; Nürnberg 5-10 min after she stopped. (b) **The flooding clock.** A ship that is out of the fight but has a friend alongside is kept for hours and given up when the water wins (Lützow about 7 h; Warrior about 14 h; Szent István about 3 h) or the moment an enemy appears (Elbing about 3 h, Rostock about 2.5 h, Sparrowhawk about 7 h with U-boats reported). [INFERRED]
- **Who decides.** Captains decided in every German and British case. When the captain was dead, the next officer simply repeated the order (Mainz's executive officer; Kingani's chief engineer struck). **Admirals decided to leave a ship** (Hipper and Blücher; Spee's "save yourself") but not to strike her. **A consort scuttles a ship already emptied**: G38 for Lützow, V71/V73 for Rostock, Marksman for Sparrowhawk, Russian destroyers for Slava, Kerch at Novorossiysk. A **committee** overrode the captain only in the revolutionary Russian fleet (Slava). [INFERRED]
- **The German rule in practice: abandon or scuttle with the colours up.** Königsberg (flags flying as she sank) and Leipzig (battle ensign flying with no ammunition) show it; Emden too, until hours after the fight. The exceptions are Nürnberg, which struck after being stopped and fired on for 5 more min, and a launch whose captain was killed (Kingani). Even Emden, aground and silent, kept her ensign up until Sydney fired again. [INFERRED from SRC rows]
- **The British rule: never strike; destroy the books; abandon when she's sinking.** Monmouth turned on Nürnberg; Shark fought to the last gun and abandoned only when sinking; Nestor's boats were provisioned while she was still afloat. British destroyer men taken prisoner (Nomad, Nestor, Turbulent's 13, some of Tipperary's) were picked out of the water, not taken from a struck ship. [INFERRED]
- **Firing on after a ship stopped.** It was routine while the colours flew (Nürnberg; Emden; Monmouth; Leipzig's men killed after the scuttle order; V187 when her men fired at the rescuers). **Firing stopped on a signal the enemy could read**: flares (Leipzig), struck colours (Nürnberg), a white flag (Dresden, Emden). [INFERRED]
- **Survivors' shares** split by who stays and by the water. With the victor stopping in warm or temperate water, many get off: Mainz 80%, Emden 58%, Rostock and Elbing nearly all, Warrior and Lützow nearly all by transfer. In the South Atlantic in December, or with the victor gone or driven off, very few: Nürnberg 7, Leipzig 18, Cöln 1, Wiesbaden 1, Monmouth 0, Scharnhorst 0, Shark 6-7. [INFERRED]

## Q2. Written orders on surrender and scuttling

### Takeaway
British and US law punished a captain who gave up a ship that could still be defended, or who struck "without proper authority", with death at most. Neither demanded scuttling by statute; that was practice, and the records (books destroyed, the Novorossiysk charges) show it. A German written order against striking was not found in this pass, but German practice was uniform.

### Cited findings
- **Naval Discipline Act 1866, s. 2** (in force 1914-18). Any captain "who shall surrender his Ship to the Enemy when capable of making a successful Defence", or "in Time of Action shall improperly withdraw from the Fight", or fails to "use his utmost Exertion to bring his Ship into Action", shall suffer death if he acted traitorously, death or less from cowardice, and dismissal from negligence. **s. 3**: forsaking station, or failing to "relieve and assist a known Friend in View to the utmost of his Power". — [NDA 1866 text, pdavis.nl](https://pdavis.nl/NDA1866.htm) [SRC]
- **US Articles for the Government of the Navy (1862, in force through WWI), Art. 3, Eighth**: death or court-martial for anyone who "shall strike, or attempt to strike, the flag to an enemy or rebel without proper authority, or when engaged in battle shall treacherously yield or pusillanimously cry for quarter". **Tenth**: not doing "his utmost to overtake and capture or destroy any vessel which it is his duty to encounter". **Sec. 14**: after a loss, pay continues if officers and crew "did their utmost to preserve her". — [Articles of War 1862, usnlp.org](https://usnlp.org/Articles_of_War1862/index.html) [SRC]
- **King's Regulations and Admiralty Instructions 1913**: the table of contents has no chapter on surrender or destroying one's ship. The relevant headings are "National Colours" (ch. II), "In case of Wreck" and "Logs, Register and Signal Books" (ch. XIII), and "Prisoners of War" (ch. XLIX). — [KR&AI 1913 index](https://freepages.rootsweb.com/~pbtyc/genealogy/KR&AI/Index.html) [SRC]
- **The custom.** Striking the ensign is "conclusive evidence of a surrender" for a warship. Firing on after a strike was an offence unless the ship showed otherwise by firing or running. A white flag asks for a parley; it is not in itself a surrender. — [WP, Striking the colors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Striking_the_colors) [SRC]. **In the record**: Glasgow fired on Dresden until her white flag went up, and her parley was used to win time for the charges [SRC, WP Dresden]. Sydney re-opened fire on beached Emden because her colours flew and she gave no answer [SRC, Glossop].
- **Russian court-martial for loss through negligence**: Zhemchug's captain got 3.5 years (commuted) and lost rank and nobility. — [WP, Zhemchug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_cruiser_Zhemchug) [SRC]
- **The Russian scuttling method in 1918**: Kingston valves alone were rejected "because of the risk of quick enemy salvage" (the 1904-05 experience); engine-room demolition charges plus open Kingstons were used. — [WP, Tsemes Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinking_of_ships_of_the_Black_Sea_Fleet_in_Tsemes_Bay) [SRC]

### Inferences
- **The law's test is "capable of making a successful defence".** For a game this maps cleanly onto "the ship can still fight": a captain who strikes while guns and speed remain is court-martialled. Once she cannot fight, nothing in the British or US code obliges her to sink rather than strike. The near-total refusal to strike in 1914-18 is culture and doctrine more than law. [INFERRED]

### Gaps
- **German orders were not found**: the Kaiser's or the Admiralstab's instructions on not striking and on scuttling to avoid capture (1914), Scheer's orders, and the Marineordnung's wording. Sources to search: Groos/Raeder *Der Krieg zur See*, the Dienstvorschriften, de.wikipedia (the "Flagge streichen" page does not exist).
- The US Navy Regulations (1913/1920) articles on a commanding officer's duty to destroy codes or sink a ship in danger of capture were not reached (the ibiblio "Rocks & Shoals" page is blocked to robots).
- RN Admiralty instructions on destroying confidential books, beyond Bingham's practice on Nestor [SRC], were not read.

## Q3. Synthesis: the triggers by navy and type, as ranges for a game

### Takeaway
Triggers for the step, by navy:
- **Strike** (minor or beaten navies only, and Germans or British only in extremity): **all guns silent and the enemy closing at short range**.
- **Abandon or scuttle in the fight**: **the last gun out or the last round fired**, within about 5-50 min.
- **Hold on**: a ship with a friend alongside holds on for hours. She is scuttled when **an enemy is sighted** (at once) or **the water wins** (about 2-14 h).
- **Russian 1917-18**: the government or the crew scuttles to deny the ship to an enemy.

### Cited findings (the rows above, by trigger)
- **Last gun or ammunition → abandon or scuttle:** Königsberg (1 gun at 12:53 → abandon 13:40), Leipzig (out of ammunition → scuttle order 19:20), Gneisenau (out at 17:15 → sank 18:02), Emden (1 gun at 11:00 → beached 11:20), Mainz (most guns out by 13:20 → abandon about 13:30). — rows 1b, 1c [SRC]
- **Stopped and silent, still under fire → strike:** Nürnberg (stopped 18:35, fired on 5 more min, struck); Avnillah (afire, struck at 35 min); Kingani (captain killed, struck within 11 min). — rows 1a-1c [SRC]
- **Enemy sighted while a cripple is being saved → scuttle at once:** Elbing (destroyers at 03:00), Rostock (Dublin at 03:55, about 30 min to the sinking), Sparrowhawk (U-boat reports, tow failed). — row 1d [SRC]
- **Flooding beyond saving with a friend alongside → transfer and scuttle:** Lützow (8,000 t, forward draught 17 m), Warrior (upper deck 4 ft above water in a rising sea), Slava (draught too deep for the channel). — rows 1d, 1e [SRC]
- **The admiral gives up a cripple** (no strike): Blücher (Hipper); Spee releasing Gneisenau. — rows 1b, 1c [SRC]
- **Denial without battle:** Novorossiysk 1918 (an 8-day order); Dresden (scuttled during a parley). — rows 1c, 1e [SRC]

- **Conditions for a strike in the German and British navies, at their most generous:** no gun firing, stopped (or aground), an enemy at close range still firing after the ship fell silent, and no friend near. Even then about half the cases (Leipzig, Monmouth, Emden at first) kept the colours up and chose death or scuttling. A game value: a **0-30% chance to strike once all of those hold**, higher for a small craft whose captain is dead, much higher (about 70% or more) for Ottoman and other minor navies of 1911-13. [EST]
- **Abandon or scuttle decision delay after the trigger:** in action, a median of about 20 min (range 5-50). With a friend alongside, a median of about 3 h (range 2-14 h), cut to 0-10 min on an enemy sighting. **Scuttling means and time to go:** a consort's torpedoes, about 2-30 min (Lützow 2, Rostock about 30 incl. charges, Svobodnaya Rossiya 4 after the fourth torpedo); charges, about 15-30 min (Cöln about 14, Dresden about 30); one torpedo in six worked at Slava. [INFERRED from SRC]
- **Share of crew killed at the moment of decision:** Mainz 20%, Königsberg about 20% (of 322 [EST]), Emden about 40-50% (half killed or wounded), Lützow about 14%, V187 about 29%. So **casualties are not the trigger**. The guns, the water and the enemy's approach are; the men left are what the guns need. [INFERRED]
