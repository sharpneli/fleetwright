# Signs of surrender at sea, the law, and the delay to cease fire (1890-1925)

## Q1. What were the signs of surrender, and what did the law and manuals say makes a surrender complete?

### Takeaway
At sea the one conclusive sign was **the ensign hauled down**. Hauled down on purpose, it had to be seen: a flag shot away did not count, and neither did a second ensign still flying elsewhere. The white flag strictly asked for a parley, not a surrender. An international code hoist (Nebogatov's XGE) was not trusted by itself. The case law of 1905 and 1914 adds a working rule, given in no treaty: **a ship must also stop and stop firing.** A ship still under way or still firing was taken as not yet surrendered, whatever flag she showed. The written law of the period (Hague 1907, Oxford Manual 1913) forbids killing those who have laid down their arms and sinking a surrendered ship before her crew is off. It never defines the moment a warship's surrender is complete, so in practice the shooting side's captain decided.

### Cited Findings
**Signs used in the record**
- **Colours hauled down (the standard sign):** at the Falklands, Kent stopped firing on Nürnberg "on the colours being hauled down" [SRC] — [Sturdee's despatch](https://dcmpx.remotevs.com/org/gwpda/www/PL/naval/j06falkd.htm). At Santiago, Texas ceased fire on a Spanish cruiser when she "struck its colors" (11:10) [SRC]. Colón "hauled down her colors, leaving them on deck at the foot of her flagstaff" (13:20) [SRC] — [Philip to Sampson, 4 July 1898 (NHHC)](https://www.history.navy.mil/research/publications/documentary-histories/united-states-navy-s/the-battle-of-santia/captain-john-w-phili.html).
- **A white flag at Santiago was read as surrender and fire stopped:** at 10:35 a Spanish ship "sheered toward shore and ran up a white flag", and Texas ceased fire on her [SRC] — [Philip (NHHC)](https://www.history.navy.mil/research/publications/documentary-histories/united-states-navy-s/the-battle-of-santia/captain-john-w-phili.html). (NHHC's footnote says Philip had the Spanish ships' order wrong, so his ships can't be named reliably [SRC].)
- **The international signal, Tsushima, 28 May 1905:** Nebogatov hoisted the international surrender signal. Wikipedia gives it as **XGE**. The [russojapanesewar.com page](https://losthistory.net/russojapanesewar/xgh.html) gives it as **XGH**, at a caption time of 10:15 [UNCERTAIN: the letters and the time differ by source]. According to the [Battle of Tsushima article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Tsushima):
  - The Japanese kept firing because their code books lacked a "surrender" signal, and they had to look one up [SRC].
  - Nebogatov then sent up a **white tablecloth**. Tōgō read it as a request for a truce or parley, not a legal surrender, and noted that either way it did not stop the ships [SRC].
  - The codebook showed that XGE required engines stopped. With the ships still moving, Tōgō kept firing while the "STOP" reply was hoisted [SRC].
  - Nebogatov **lowered the St Andrew's ensign, raised the Japanese flag on the gaff, and stopped all engines**. Only then did Tōgō cease fire and accept [SRC].
  - The russojapanesewar.com page says Tōgō ceased fire once "satisfied no trick was being attempted" [SRC].
- **Signalled demand and answer, Cocos, 9 Nov 1914:** Sydney found the beached Emden's battle ensign still flying, which usually means she will fight on. She signalled "Do you surrender?" by lamp and flag hoist. Emden replied "What signal? No signal books." The demand was repeated in plain Morse, then "Have you received my signal?", with no reply. Sydney fired two salvoes. Emden's ensign was then hauled down and burned, and a **white sheet hoisted over the quarterdeck** [SRC] — [Battle of Cocos (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cocos).
- **Hauled down plus a white handkerchief, Lake Tanganyika, 26 Dec 1915:** Kingani's commander (Sub-Lt Junge) was killed by a 3-pdr shell through the gunshield. The chief engineer then "hauled down the colours" and waved a white handkerchief, and Toutou came alongside and took possession [SRC] — [HMS Fifi (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Fifi). Taken with Emden's white sheet, this suggests that by 1914-15 a white cloth went up beside the lowered ensign as a second, plainer sign [INFERRED].
- **Several ensigns: one down is not enough.** As Gneisenau sank (5:40 pm), "the flag flying at her fore truck was apparently hauled down, but at the peak continued flying". The British cease-fire came at 5:50 [SRC] — [Sturdee](https://dcmpx.remotevs.com/org/gwpda/www/PL/naval/j06falkd.htm). Warships in action flew several battle ensigns [INFERRED from the fore truck and peak], so an observer had to see every one come down.
- **The opposite sign, colours kept flying to the last:** Scharnhorst's "flag remained flying to the last" [SRC]. Nürnberg's men waved a German ensign on a staff as she sank [SRC] — [Sturdee](https://dcmpx.remotevs.com/org/gwpda/www/PL/naval/j06falkd.htm). Ushakov at Tsushima did not accept the signalled news of the surrender, fought while fleeing, and was scuttled, her captain refusing Japanese help [SRC] — [Battle of Tsushima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Tsushima).

**Law and manuals**
- **Oxford Manual of Naval War (Institut de droit international, 1913):**
  - Art. 17 forbids "to kill or to wound an enemy who, having laid down his arms" or having surrendered at discretion.
  - Art. 17 also forbids sinking a ship that has surrendered before her crew has been taken off, and declaring that no quarter will be given.
  - Art. 15 forbids treacherous ruses, including "to make improper use of a flag of truce" and "to make use of false flags, uniforms, or insignia".
  - Art. 45 treats a ship sent to parley under a white flag as a cartel ship.
  - Art. 85 says that after every engagement belligerents shall search for the shipwrecked and wounded, as far as military interests allow.
  - The Manual has **no article on how or when a warship's surrender is complete**. The only flag-lowering it mentions is for detained hospital ships (Art. 41). [SRC] — [Oxford Manual 1913, ICRC text](https://lagen.nu/icrc/265)
- **Customary rule as summarised by Wikipedia (citing Halleck 1861 and Colombos 1972):**
  - For a warship, striking colours is conclusive evidence of surrender. For a merchant ship it is not.
  - It is an offence to fight on after striking. It is also an offence to keep firing on a ship that has struck, unless she shows by her acts (firing on, trying to escape) that she has not truly surrendered.
  - A white flag asks for a truce to communicate. It is not a surrender.
  - A warship must fly her own ensign before any hostile act.
  - What would be perfidy for a warship (striking as a ruse) is not perfidy for a merchant ship, which may strike to escape capture.

  [SRC, secondary] — [Striking the colors (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Striking_the_colors)
- **Hague 1907:** the Hague Regulations (annexed to Convention IV) Art. 23(c), (d) and (f) forbid killing or wounding an enemy who has laid down his arms or has no means of defence left and has surrendered at discretion. They also forbid declaring no quarter, and improper use of a flag of truce, the national flag or the enemy's insignia. Convention X (the Geneva rules adapted to war at sea) Art. 16 requires a search for the shipwrecked and wounded after each engagement, as far as military interests permit. [SRC, from the treaty texts; quoted from the known wording, not re-fetched this session: verify against [Avalon, Hague X](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/hague10.asp)]. Neither convention defines a warship's surrender either [INFERRED].

### Inferences
- **What the record treated as a complete surrender:**
  1. Every ensign down, deliberately (or a white cloth in its place).
  2. The ship stopped (Tōgō's condition, 1905).
  3. Her guns silent (Gneisenau, Emden).
  4. No ruse feared (Tōgō's "no trick"; Glossop's fear of torpedoes or small arms).

  Each of these was a separate thing an observer had to see. The game's existing observer flags (quiet, stopped) cover 2 and 3. A new "colours down" fact is needed for 1. [INFERRED]
- **Guns trained fore and aft, men mustered, boats lowered:** I found no period source treating these as formal signs of surrender. They show up as evidence of abandonment, not of striking. [INFERRED; see Gaps]

### Gaps
- The US Naval War Code (Stockton, 1900) text was not reached. From memory it requires the national colours to be shown before firing and forbids false colours in action [UNCERTAIN, unverified]. Its rules on surrender, if any, were not checked.
- Oppenheim vol. II (1906/1912), Higgins (1909), and the RN and German instructions on accepting surrender were not reached in this budget.
- Whether the international code of 1901 really had a "surrender" group (XGE vs XGH) is unresolved.

## Q2. How long from the sign to cease fire, and why the delays?

### Takeaway
Where a captain saw a clear sign, fire stopped **at once or within about 5 minutes**. Santiago 1898 and Kent on Nürnberg's hauled-down colours are examples. The long delays came from an **unclear sign**, not from the shooters being slow to react:
- An ensign still flying, at Cocos (some minutes of signalling, then two salvoes) and on Nürnberg (about 5 min of renewed fire).
- An unfamiliar signal on a ship still under way, Nebogatov's (tens of minutes [UNCERTAIN]).
- A ship that stopped and then fired again, Gneisenau (20 min from "appeared stopped" to "Cease fire").

The reasons in the record were flags still flying, the ship moving, guns firing again, signals not understood and fear of a trick. Smoke and range as causes are suggested by the record but not shown in the sources reached.

- **Visibility as context:** at the Falklands the weather changed after 4 pm and visibility was much reduced, and smoke from shell fire and fires rose everywhere on the stopped Gneisenau [SRC] — [Sturdee](https://dcmpx.remotevs.com/org/gwpda/www/PL/naval/j06falkd.htm). The despatch does not say this delayed the cease-fire.

### Inferences
- **Times for the game** [EST]:
  - A clear sign seen at battle range (every ensign down, or a white flag on a ship stopping): 0-5 min to cease fire. Kent's case gives about 5 min.
  - A partial or doubtful sign (one ensign down, a signal hoist only, or still moving): 10-30 min. Gneisenau gives 20 min, Nebogatov tens of minutes.
  - No sign but a silent burning wreck: fire stops when the captain judges her done. Leipzig's case is in line with crew.md N5b's 3-6 min quiet.
- **A sign of life overrides a sign of surrender:** Gneisenau firing again cancelled the cease-fire [SRC], which matches crew.md decision 3. Tōgō's rule (still under way means not surrendered) argues that "stopped" should be a prerequisite before a strike is believed [INFERRED].
- **Nürnberg's case gives a firm rule:** a ship gone quiet but with colours still flying draws fire again until they come down [SRC]. For BL5: a screen keeps attacking a cripple until her colours are seen down, and "seen" needs the ensign within an observer's sight at that range [INFERRED].

### Gaps
- No minute-by-minute timing of Nebogatov's surrender was reached (Tōgō's report, Westwood, Corbett's Maritime Operations). The commonly cited window of about 10:30 to 10:53 is from memory [UNCERTAIN; verify].
- No source reached measured how far a struck ensign could be seen. In the period it was read by telescope; the range is unknown [UNCERTAIN].

## Q3. Ships fired on after striking or while abandoning, and ships wrongly taken for struck

### Takeaway
In the sourced cases, fire after a sign mostly came because the sign was incomplete: Emden's and Nürnberg's colours still up, Gneisenau firing again. Proven firing on men known to have surrendered is rare in the sources reached; the Baralong case is the clear one, and it is tied to a ruse. Ships that "stopped then fired again" (Gneisenau) and wrecks that kept a gun firing (Suvorov) are the record's warning against believing a quiet ship has given up.

### Cited Findings
- **Emden:** about 20 killed by Sydney's two salvoes, fired because her ensign still flew and she did not answer the signals. Glossop reckoned she could still use torpedoes or small arms against boarders [SRC] — [Battle of Cocos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cocos). The article gives two figures for Emden's dead, 134 and 130 [UNCERTAIN].
- **Nürnberg:** Kent reopened fire on a ship that had ceased firing and was burning, for about 5 min, until her colours came down. 12 men were picked up and 7 survived [SRC] — [Sturdee](https://dcmpx.remotevs.com/org/gwpda/www/PL/naval/j06falkd.htm).
- **Gneisenau:** appeared stopped at 5:30 and a cease fire was ordered, but she opened fire again before it was hoisted [SRC] — same.
- **Suvorov at Tsushima:** "while she had a gun above water she fired"; destroyers finished her [SRC] — [Battle of Tsushima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Tsushima).
- **Nebogatov's ships fired on after the first surrender signal:** the Japanese kept firing through XGE and the tablecloth until the ships stopped and showed the Japanese flag [SRC] — same. The minutes and the casualties of that interval were not found [UNCERTAIN].
- **Izumrud:** escaped during the lull of the surrender [SRC] — [losthistory](https://losthistory.net/russojapanesewar/xgh.html); [Nebogatov (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Nebogatov). A surrender of a squadron covering a ship's escape is the kind of thing that made captors wary [INFERRED].
- **Baralong, 19 Aug 1915 (U-27):** Baralong closed under the US ensign, hauled it down and hoisted the White Ensign before opening fire at about 600 yd. U-27 sank after 34 rounds. Twelve men survived the sinking and 4-6 reached the Nicosian; the German memorandum says the rest were shot clinging to the boat falls and four were killed in Nicosian's engine room. Herbert justified it by the fear that they would scuttle her. **24 Sept 1915 (U-41):** Baralong again under the US flag, opened fire at about 700 yd; two U-41 men survived. Germany alleged a lifeboat was run down, which the British denied [SRC] — [Baralong incidents (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baralong_incidents).
- **Ushakov:** refused the signalled surrender and fought on [SRC] — [Battle of Tsushima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Tsushima). Her case is a refusal to surrender, not fire on a struck ship.

### Inferences
- **For the game:** a ship whose colours are down, that is stopped and silent, should not be fired on. A doctrine field could let a side be harsher (Tōgō's caution) or quicker to believe [INFERRED]. Firing on a struck ship stays a game event (an incident), not an AI habit [INFERRED].

### Gaps (leads not verified in this budget)
- **Wiesbaden at Jutland:** disabled, she lay between the fleets and was fired on by many British ships through the evening; one survivor (Zenne). Whether she ever struck is not known to me. Not checked against a source this session.
- **Blücher at Dogger Bank:** the WFA summary says only that hundreds drowned and rescue was called off because of a Zeppelin attack [SRC] — [WFA "I Was There"](https://www.westernfrontassociation.com/i-was-there/2015/january/i-saw-beatty-triumph-the-bluecher-sunk-by-an-officer-of-hms-aurora-24-january-1915-episode-56/). The full accounts (whether she showed a sign, whether she fired to the end, the seaplane and Zeppelin bombing the rescuers) were not reached.
- **Leipzig's lights:** other accounts say she fired two green lights, taken as a surrender sign. Sturdee's despatch does not mention them [UNCERTAIN].
- **Santiago:** accounts of Vizcaya, Oquendo and Teresa being fired on after the white flags (Evans's "don't cheer" and others) were not reached; Philip's report records no firing after a surrender [SRC].
- **Ships wrongly taken for sunk or struck:** no sourced case was found in this budget (for instance, a flag shot away and read as struck). Lützow, Seydlitz and Derfflinger reported as sunk at Jutland are known leads, from memory [UNCERTAIN].

## Q4. Ruses and false flags: did they bear on whether a sign was believed?

### Takeaway
The period's law allowed false colours **up to the first shot**. The ship had to show her own ensign before firing. Merchant ships could strike falsely, warships could not. Baralong showed the rule in use, the true ensign hoisted just before opening fire. Ruses of this sort and the memory of Izumrud's escape were the background to Tōgō's "no trick" caution and Glossop's two salvoes, though no source reached ties them directly.

### Cited Findings
- **The ensign before hostile acts:** a warship must fly her ensign before any hostile act. Striking as a ruse is perfidy for a warship but not for a merchant ship [SRC, secondary] — [Striking the colors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Striking_the_colors).
- **The Oxford Manual is stricter:** Art. 15 forbids the use of false flags, uniforms or insignia outright, not only in action [SRC] — [Oxford Manual 1913](https://lagen.nu/icrc/265).
- **Baralong in practice:** under the US flag, then the White Ensign hoisted before opening fire; Germany protested the misuse of neutral colours [SRC] — [Baralong incidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baralong_incidents).
- **Tōgō waited** until satisfied "no trick was being attempted" [SRC] — [losthistory](https://losthistory.net/russojapanesewar/xgh.html).
- **Glossop feared** torpedoes or small arms from a beached ship whose ensign still flew [SRC] — [Battle of Cocos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cocos).

### Inferences
- **Trust in a sign of surrender** could be a side's or navy's standing: lower after ruses, and lower for Q-ship-era submarines and merchant ships [INFERRED].
- **Emden's fourth funnel** was a disguise of her shape, not her colours; no source reached ties it to the trust in her surrender [INFERRED].

### Gaps
- Emden's dummy funnel and the Q-ship rules were not researched in this budget, beyond Baralong.
- No source reached says how the RN or German navy instructed officers to treat a sign after a ruse.
