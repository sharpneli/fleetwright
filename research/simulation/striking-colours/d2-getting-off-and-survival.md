# Getting the crew off and keeping them alive, 1890-1925: mechanisms and numbers

Scope note: this strand covers mechanisms and rates (boats and floats, leaving the ship, survival in the water, rescue). Individual sinking cases appear only where they supply a rate. Tags: [SRC] figure stated by the cited source; [INFERRED] reasoned from cited figures; [EST] a modelling estimate for the game, with no direct source; [UNCERTAIN] remembered or weakly sourced, to be checked. The session's web budget was spent on the physiology and the launching analogues, so the navy-by-navy kit question (Q1) and escape rates (Q2) are thin and mostly sit in Gaps.

## 1. Boats and floats carried; launching them, with and without a list

### Takeaway
Warships of the period carried boats for well under the whole crew. In action the boats were often unusable: smashed by blast or splinters, or impossible to hoist out once the steam winches failed. The Carley float (patented 1903) was the main float, and its largest model held about 50 men, roughly half of them in the water holding on. A list of a few degrees already made launching boats on one side difficult, and 15° (Lusitania) effectively ruled out the high side. Even with an intact ship, launching under way went at about one boat per 5 minutes overall, or one per 5-10 minutes on each side (Titanic).

### Cited Findings
- Carley float: designed by Horace Carley, US patent 734,118 (filed 1902, granted 1903) [SRC]. It is a copper or steel tube 12-20 in (30-50 cm) in diameter, bent into an oval, wrapped in kapok or cork and covered in painted canvas [SRC]. Baffles divide the tube into watertight compartments, so it floats either side up even with the cover holed [SRC]. The floor is wood slats or webbing, with paddles, water and rations lashed to it [SRC]. Some had a calcium flare that lit on contact with water [SRC]. — [Wikipedia: Carley float](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carley_float)
- Largest Carley model: up to 50 men, about half inside the ring and the rest in the water holding the rope loops round the edge [SRC]. Used by several navies in both world wars [SRC]. — [Wikipedia: Carley float](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carley_float)
- WWI use: at Zeebrugge (1918), Lt Bonham Carter escaped the scuttled blockship Intrepid on a Carley float. Its flare could have drawn fire, but the burning ship's smoke hid it [SRC]. — [Wikipedia: Carley float](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carley_float)
- Denton floats and rafts (WWI, War Office pattern): one forum contributor describes them as about 3 ft square with tarred rope loops, too small to climb onto, so men could only hang on [UNCERTAIN: forum recollection]. A WWII memoir calls one "virtually a piece of cork about 18in x 18ins with ropes on each side" [SRC, memoir quoted on the forum]. The same thread suggests ships with boats for the whole crew may have carried no floats [UNCERTAIN]. SOLAS 1914 followed the Titanic but gave no specifics for warships [SRC, forum]. — [Great War Forum: life-saving floats and rafts, naval vessels WW1](https://www.greatwarforum.org/topic/295568-life-saving-floats-and-rafts-naval-vessels-ww1/)
- Boats lost to damage: only one of Aboukir's lifeboats could be launched (22 Sep 1914), because of explosion damage and failed steam-powered winches [SRC]. — [Wikipedia: Action of 22 September 1914](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_of_22_September_1914)
- Lusitania (1915): about 15° list to starboard [SRC, Turner Q98, Q109]. The port boats "caught on the rail and capsized" some people, and others were "let go on the run" [SRC, Q110]. She was making 18 kn when struck. Turner ordered full astern and told the crew to hold the boats until "the way was off the ship a bit" [SRC, Q85, Q103, Q106]. She sank about 18 minutes after the torpedo [SRC, Q113]. — [Lusitania inquiry, Turner day 1](https://www.titanicinquiry.org/Lusitania/01turner1.php)
- Lusitania inquiry report: about twenty minutes from alarm to sinking. The engines were disabled almost at once, so the ship kept moving through the launching [SRC]. The list made it impossible to launch the port boats properly: they swung inboard [SRC]. The starboard boats hung inconveniently far outboard, and passengers found them very hard to board [SRC]. Some port boats were damaged being pushed over the side and leaked in the water. Many starboard boats were lowered, and there is no good evidence that any of them leaked [SRC]. — [Lusitania Wreck Commissioner's report, boats](https://www.titanicinquiry.org/Lusitania/Report/Repboats.php)
- Titanic (1912, calm sea, no damage to the boat deck): 16 lifeboats were launched in about 70-75 minutes (12:40/12:45 to 1:50/1:55), plus 4 collapsibles, the last two floating off at about 2:15 [SRC]. That is an average of about 4.7 minutes between boats across both sides, mostly every 5-10 minutes on each side, with gaps of up to 25 minutes [SRC]. — [Lifeboats, launch times, list and trim, part 1 (titanicology / Encyclopedia Titanica)](https://www.titanicology.com/Titanica/LifeboatLaunchTimesList%26TrimPart1.pdf)
- Titanic list effects: with a starboard list of about 2° or more, boat 6 on the port side rubbed the hull and had to be pushed off while lowering [SRC]. With a port list of about 10°, boat 10 on the port side hung 2½ ft clear of the ship, and children had to be passed across the gap [SRC]. Boats on the side away from the list could rub the hull even with the davits swung out [SRC]. Some lowerings went wrong: an after fall snagged on boat 14, and boat 15 came down almost on top of boat 13 [SRC]. One or two ABs often lowered a boat [SRC, partial]. — [titanicology part 1](https://www.titanicology.com/Titanica/LifeboatLaunchTimesList%26TrimPart1.pdf)

### Inferences
- Launching by list [INFERRED from Titanic and Lusitania, with EST thresholds]:
  - 0-2°: both sides launch normally.
  - About 3-5°: boats on the high side rub and hang up and need pushing off; launches on that side take about 1.5-2x as long. On the low side the boats swing out of reach, so boarding is slow.
  - About 10°: on the low side, a 2-3 ft gap; boarding needs jumping or passing people across. High-side launching is largely impractical.
  - 15° and more: the high side is out entirely (Lusitania). The low side works only if the falls and davits are manned and the ship has stopped.
  Way on the ship (Lusitania kept moving through the launching) is a separate multiplier: boats lowered at speed swamp or capsize.
- Battle damage [EST]: the share of boats usable after action depends on the hits taken. Aboukir, hit by a single torpedo, launched one boat, because power davits and winches died with the steam. A rule for ships of 1890-1925: boats need the hoisting gear (steam or electric winch, derrick or boom). With power gone, only the davit boats (cutters, whalers, gigs: 20-30% of boat capacity) can be lowered by hand; boats stowed on crutches under the main derrick cannot be hoisted out.
- Launch rate for a warship, intact and stopped [EST]: about 1 davit boat every 2-3 minutes per side, with a drilled crew of 6-10 per boat. That is faster than Titanic, whose crew was untrained and half-filled the boats; but the big boats need the derrick, at 5-10 minutes each [EST].
- Float capacity per Carley float [SRC for the largest model]. Game sizes [EST]: about 20-50 men "carried", of whom about half sit in it and are partly protected from cold, and the rest are in the water holding on, at the immersion rate.

## 2. How fast a crew can leave the ship

### Takeaway
No period drill timings or measured hatch throughputs were found. The sourced cases bound the time available: a single-torpedo armoured cruiser capsized in 10-25 minutes, a liner with a 15° list sank in 18-20 minutes, and a battered cruiser capsized after a final torpedo salvo and floated upside down "for several minutes". A capsize traps whoever is below. The game will need estimated throughput rates.

### Cited Findings
- Aboukir capsized 25 minutes after the torpedo and sank 5 minutes later. Hogue, hit twice, capsized after 10 minutes. Cressy was hit at about 07:25 and 07:30 and floated capsized until 07:55. Hogue's captain ordered abandon ship within about 5 minutes of the hit [SRC]. Of the three ships, 1,459 were killed and 837 saved [SRC]. — [Wikipedia: Action of 22 September 1914](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_of_22_September_1914)
- Blücher, after 70-100 heavy shells and several torpedoes, capsized at 13:13 after a point-blank torpedo salvo and floated upside down for several minutes before sinking [SRC]. Survivors: 234 (British documents), 260 (Hildebrand et al.) or 6 officers and 275 men (Schmalenbach), out of about 999 (Schmalenbach) or at least 1,200 (Goldrick) [SRC]. — [Wikipedia: SMS Blücher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Bl%C3%BCcher)
- Lusitania: about 18-20 minutes from the hit to sinking, at a 15° list [SRC]. — [Turner testimony](https://www.titanicinquiry.org/Lusitania/01turner1.php); [Lusitania report](https://www.titanicinquiry.org/Lusitania/Report/Repboats.php)

### Inferences
- Throughput [EST, for the game]:
  - A vertical ladder or steep companion: about 15-25 men a minute while the ship is level, in order and lit; falling to about 5-10 a minute in the dark, under smoke or with wounded.
  - The rate falls with heel, about linearly to zero at 35-45°, beyond which ladders and hatches become walls and ceilings.
  - Hatches are doubled up with ladders in the main routes; a stokehold or engine room usually has 2-4 escapes (ladders up through the uptake casing or the gratings).
- Share of each space who get out [EST]:
  - Upper deck and superstructure: about 90-100% reach the water before she goes, if the time to sink exceeds about 3 minutes.
  - Stations one deck below: about 60-80%.
  - Engine and boiler rooms: about 10-40% in a slow sinking (10-30 min); about 0-5% in a fast capsize (under 3 min) or a magazine explosion.
  - Magazines, shell rooms and handing rooms (deepest, behind armoured hatches and flash doors): about 0-10% unless the order comes early.
- Capsize vs foundering [INFERRED]: a ship that rolls over (Aboukir, Hogue, Blücher) traps nearly everyone still below at the moment of the roll. Those on deck are thrown off or walk down the bottom. A ship that founders on an even keel gives time to leave by every exit. The game's risk to men below should therefore be driven by the time to capsize, not the time to sink.
- Jumpers vs those carried down [EST]: of the men on deck at the plunge, about 10-30% are pulled down by suction, struck by debris, funnels and masts, or caught in rigging. Higher for men who stay on the high side and walk off late; lower for men who jump early on the low side.
- Abandon-ship order timing [INFERRED]: Hogue's captain ordered abandon ship within 5 minutes of the hit. A plausible game delay between "she won't float" and "abandon ship" is 2-10 minutes, ordered by the captain's judgement.

### Gaps
- No measured men-per-minute rates for hatches or ladders from the period. Modern IMO evacuation data (e.g. MSC/Circ.1238, stair flow about 1.1 persons/m/s) would anchor the level-ship rate; not fetched this session.
- No RN or other abandon-ship drill timings for 1890-1925 found.
- No dataset on the share of each department surviving (stokers vs deck hands). Case lists (the other strand) could give this by trade.

## 3. Survival in the water by temperature

### Takeaway
Cold water kills in stages. The first minutes bring cold shock: a gasp and hyperventilation, with drowning and heart rhythm disturbance, worst below about 15 °C. Swimming fails within minutes to tens of minutes. Hypothermia follows over hours. The classic rule of thumb: about half die within an hour at 10 °C (50 °F). The USCG/USN-style table gives "death time" bands from 15-45 minutes at 0 °C to effectively unlimited above 27 °C. Wartime experience says cold killed most of those who reached the water.

### Cited Findings
- Robertson (Res Medica 1964), reviewing the cold-water literature: "one hour at 50 °F can be expected to kill about 50%" [SRC]. Few survive a rectal temperature fall below 78.5 °F (25.8 °C) [SRC]. — [Robertson, Survival in Cold Water, Res Medica](https://journals.ed.ac.uk/resmedica/article/download/419/703/1560)
- Robertson: cold immersion accounted for perhaps two-thirds of wartime sea deaths [SRC, but Robertson gives no source; likely from McCance et al.] — [Res Medica](https://journals.ed.ac.uk/resmedica/article/download/419/703/1560)
- Molnar (1946, JAMA) used US Navy Bureau of Medicine and Surgery records of cases with both the water temperature and the time immersed. The survival boundary is roughly a hyperbola: the temperature deficit times the survival time is about constant [SRC]. — [Res Medica](https://journals.ed.ac.uk/resmedica/article/download/419/703/1560)
- Rectal temperature fall in the first hour (Spealman): about 2 °F/h in 75 °F (24 °C) water, about 5 °F/h in 55 °F (13 °C), about 12 °F/h in 45 °F (7 °C) [SRC]. Above about 60-68 °F (16-20 °C), the core settles at a lower level within the first hour, with heat production 3-6 times normal [SRC]. — [Res Medica](https://journals.ed.ac.uk/resmedica/article/download/419/703/1560)
- Kritchley (1943): a ship torpedoed in water of about 29 °F (−2 °C). Only one man in ten survived more than half an hour in the water [SRC]. — [Res Medica](https://journals.ed.ac.uk/resmedica/article/download/419/703/1560)
- Cold shock: at 15 °C, end-tidal pCO₂ fell an average 1.8 mm Hg in the first 4 minutes, and at 5 °C by 4.5 mm Hg (hyperventilation), largely recovered by 20 minutes [SRC]. Extra heartbeats occurred at 15 °C and below; ventricular fibrillation was suggested as a cause of sudden death on immersion [SRC]. Keatinge (1959): at 5 and 15 °C, work (swimming, struggling) sped up the fall in core temperature; above 25 °C it slowed it [SRC]. — [Res Medica](https://journals.ed.ac.uk/resmedica/article/download/419/703/1560)
- Clothing: clothed subjects cooled more slowly, especially when keeping still, even in non-waterproof clothes [SRC]. Fat: after 30 minutes at 15 °C, a 10 mm skin fold lost about 0.5 °C of core temperature, and a 5 mm fold about 2 °C, four times as much [SRC]. A heavy meal about 45 minutes before immersion raised the risk of cardiac irregularity [SRC]. — [Res Medica](https://journals.ed.ac.uk/resmedica/article/download/419/703/1560)
- The standard cold-water table, as commonly reproduced (the page cites no origin; it matches the USCG survival chart) [SRC, as tabulated]:

  | Water °C | Exhaustion or unconsciousness | Death time (expected survival) |
  |---|---|---|
  | 0 | under 15 min | 15-45 min |
  | 0-4 | 15-30 min | 30-90 min |
  | 4-10 | 30-60 min | 1-3 h |
  | 10-16 | 1-2 h | 1-6 h |
  | 16-21 | 2-7 h | 2-40 h |
  | 21-27 | 3-12 h | 3 h to unlimited |
  | over 27 | unlimited | unlimited |

  — [Engineering ToolBox: survival time in cold water](https://engineeringtoolbox.com/amp/survival-time-cold-water-d_1567.html); a related USCG Auxiliary sheet is at [wow.uscgaux.info](https://wow.uscgaux.info/Uploads_wowII/013-04-03/Hypothermia.pdf) (not opened)
- On floats, cold still kills: HMCS Esquimalt (April 1945, off Nova Scotia): at least 16 crew died of hypothermia in 6 hours waiting on Carley floats. A 1946 inquiry found many who reached floats still died of exposure [SRC]. — [Wikipedia: Carley float](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carley_float)
- The MRC report (McCance, Ungley, Crosfill and Widdowson, "The hazards to men in ships lost at sea, 1940-44", MRC Special Report 291, 1956) exists, and its press coverage said lifeboats were "not adequate" as a wartime safeguard [SRC: catalogue entry and headline only]. — [RCOS catalogue](https://rcosoe-apw.adlibhosting.com/AIS/Details/document/300017508); [Press, 20 Oct 1956](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/CHP19561020.2.99)

### Inferences
- Game table: survival in the water by temperature [INFERRED from the table and Robertson's 50%-in-1-h-at-10 °C, with EST interpolation]. "50%" is the time by which half of a group in the water without boats have died; "Most" is the time by which about 90% have died. These figures assume a lifebelt or something to hold. Without one, apply the swim-failure cap below.

  | 10 | 1 h | 3 h | Robertson's 50% at 1 h [SRC] |
  | 12 | 1.5 h | 4 h | North Sea in May-June |
  | 15 | 2.5 h | 6 h | Cold shock and the heart risk fade above here |

- Swim-failure cap [EST, from the Keatinge result that work speeds cooling and the cold-shock data]: a man without a lifebelt or anything to hold drowns well before hypothermia below about 15 °C. Use about 10-30 min at 0-10 °C, about 30-90 min at 10-15 °C, and about 2-4 h above 20 °C for a fit swimmer in calm water. With a lifebelt or something to hold, use the table above.
- Modifiers [EST unless marked]:
  - Clothing (working rig, jersey): about +20-50% survival time [direction SRC, size EST].
  - Out of the water on a raft or boat, wet, in wind: about 2-4x the immersion time, and at least 12 h above 10 °C.
  - Half in a Carley float: about 1.5x.
  - Night: no physiological change, but rescue finds fewer.
  - Sea state 4+: about 0.5-0.7x without a lifebelt, from repeated swamping and aspiration; less effect with one.
  - Wounded: about 0.3-0.6x.
  - Oil fuel slick (1912+ oil-fired ships): adds drowning and aspiration risk, eye and lung injury, and makes men hard to haul out (slippery). Treat as about 0.7x survival and about 0.5x pickup rate [UNCERTAIN].
  - Coal dust and coal-ship wreckage: little effect on physiology [EST].
- Deaths after pickup [SRC, cases]: Nürnberg's 11 survivors picked up by Kent's boats lost 4 to exposure, and the Gneisenau survivors Invincible picked up (108) lost 14, buried the next day (see Q4). So a share of those rescued from 5-10 °C water die after pickup, about 10-35%. Model it as "circum-rescue collapse and after-drop" [INFERRED].
- Rule of thumb for the first minutes (the "1-10-1" rule as taught: about 1 minute of cold shock, about 10 minutes of useful movement, about 1 hour before unconsciousness in ice water) [UNCERTAIN: page seen in search, not read: [SIRIUSMEDx: The 1-10-1 Had It Right](https://www.siriusmedx.com/en_CA/blog/instructor-s-blog-5/the-1-10-1-had-it-right-52)].

## 4. Rescue rates, the dangers of stopping, and sea temperatures

### Takeaway
Rescue was slow, and it was dangerous for the rescuer. Ships' boats, working in the dark after a battle, found survivors at about 15-20 men per hour per pair of ships (Leipzig). Merchant ships and trawlers on hand in daylight took off hundreds (Flora 286, Titan 147 at Aboukir). Stopping to rescue cost Hogue and Cressy. A Zeppelin's bombs broke off the British rescue at Blücher, and a raft from Fortune lost half its men overnight before rescue in the North Sea in June.

### Cited Findings
- 22 Sep 1914: of the three ships' crews, 837 were saved [SRC]. The Dutch steamer Flora rescued 286 and Titan 147; the Lowestoft trawlers Coriander and J.G.C. also rescued men [SRC]. British destroyers arrived only at 10:45, about 4½ h after the first torpedo [SRC]. Two Dutch sailing trawlers would not close Cressy for fear of mines [SRC]. Hogue and Cressy had stopped to rescue Aboukir's men when U-9 hit them [SRC]. The sea was calm after earlier heavy weather [SRC]. — [Wikipedia: Action of 22 September 1914](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_of_22_September_1914)
- Blücher (24 Jan 1915, North Sea in January): British destroyers closed to pick up men from the water. Zeppelin L5 took the sinking Blücher for a British battlecruiser and bombed the destroyers, which withdrew [SRC]. Between 234 and 281 were saved from about 1,000-1,200 [SRC]. Twenty more died as prisoners, and her commander, rescued, died of pneumonia in captivity [SRC]. — [Wikipedia: SMS Blücher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Bl%C3%BCcher)
- Falklands (8 Dec 1914): Invincible picked up 108 officers and men (Beamish's figure, placed after the Gneisenau's sinking), of whom 14 died and were buried the next day [SRC]. Kent's boats picked up 11 of Nürnberg's men, of whom 4 died of exposure [SRC]. Glasgow's and Cornwall's boats searched for Leipzig's survivors from 21:00 for just over an hour and found 18 (she sank at 21:23) [SRC]. — [Western Front Association: Battle of the Falklands 1914](https://www.westernfrontassociation.com/world-war-i-articles/battle-of-the-falklands-1914/)
- Jutland, HMS Fortune (destroyer): of 35 men clinging to rafts, 17 were rescued [SRC]. — [WFA: Ordeal by Water, CPO Hammant](https://www.westernfrontassociation.com/i-was-there/2016/may/ordeal-by-water-i-survived-when-my-destroyer-sank-by-cpo-h-hammant-31-may-1916-episode-118/)
- North Sea SST in May (modern, coastal stations): 8.6 °C (Stonehaven) to 14.2 °C (Enkhuizen). Bergen about 8-11 °C, Edinburgh about 10-11 °C, Esbjerg about 12-15 °C [SRC]. — [seatemperature.info: North Sea in May](https://seatemperature.info/may/north-sea-water-temperature.html)
- Korea Strait SST: the year-to-year spread of the seasonal cycle's amplitudes is under 1 °C (so a climatology is a fair stand-in for 1905) [SRC, abstract]. — [KoreaScience: Year-to-year fluctuations of SST in the Korea Strait](https://koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO198422105913778.page?lang=en)

### Inferences
- Pickup rates [INFERRED from the cases, EST for the rates]:
  - A ship's pulling boat (cutter or whaler, about 6-10 crew), working a field of men in the water: about 10-25 men an hour in daylight and calm water; about 5-15 at night (Leipzig: 18 found in about 1 h by two cruisers' boats, at night, after a long action).
  - Each boat makes trips of about 20-40 men before returning.
  - A merchant ship or trawler stopped among survivors in daylight: about 50-150 men an hour, with nets, ladders and lines (Flora's 286 in a morning).
  - A destroyer stopped alongside a dense field, with lines and scrambling nets over the side: about 100-200 an hour for the first half hour, falling fast as the field thins and drifts [EST].
  - A man hauled up a ship's side by line takes about 1-2 minutes, with 2-3 men on the line. Exhausted or oiled men need a boat or a man over the side.
- How long rescuers stay [EST]: in the presence of enemy ships, submarines or aircraft, they leave as soon as attacked (Blücher), so 10-30 minutes is typical in a running battle. Without threat they stay until the field is cleared, typically 1-3 h. After Hogue and Cressy, captains in submarine waters were reluctant to stop at all [INFERRED; the Admiralty order not confirmed].
- Drift and spread [EST]: men in the water spread at about 0.1-0.3 kn relative to each other in wind and sea. After an hour a field of 500 men covers about 1 km², so pickup rates fall with time.
- Sea temperatures by battle area and month [EST unless marked; modern climatology, about 0.5-1 °C warmer than 1890-1925]:
  - North Sea, late May / early June: 10-13 °C (Jutland's area about 11-12 °C) [SRC range above, central value EST].
  - North Sea, January: 4-7 °C (Dogger Bank about 5-6 °C).
  - North Sea, September (Broad Fourteens, Aboukir): 14-16 °C.
  - South Atlantic off the Falklands, December: about 7-9 °C (Falklands Current, cold even in summer).
  - Off Coronel, Chile, November: about 12-14 °C (Humboldt Current).
  - Tsushima Strait, late May: about 15-18 °C (the warm Tsushima Current, warming).
  - Yellow Sea, August (Battle of the Yellow Sea, 10 Aug 1904; Yalu, 17 Sep 1894): about 24-27 °C in August, about 22-24 °C in mid-September.
  - Off Santiago de Cuba, July: about 28 °C.
  - Mediterranean, summer: 22-26 °C. Winter: 13-16 °C.
  - Adriatic in June (Otranto Straits 1917 in May; Szent István 10 Jun 1918): about 19-22 °C.
  - Dardanelles, March: about 10-12 °C.
