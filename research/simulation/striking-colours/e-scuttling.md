## Q1. Scapa Flow, 21 June 1919: how it was done and how long each ship took

### Takeaway
Reuter's interned fleet was prepared over several days: doors, condenser covers and portholes were left open or loosened, and holes were bored through some bulkheads. When the order came at about 11:20, the crews opened sea cocks and flood valves. Large ships then took between 56 minutes (Friedrich der Grosse, 12:16) and about 5 h 40 min (Hindenburg, 17:00) to go down. Of the large ships, one battleship and three light cruisers were beached by the British. Of the 50 destroyers, about 32 sank and about 18 were beached, because they were moored closer to shore and boarding parties could reach them. Most of the sunken ships were raised between 1924 and 1939.

### Cited Findings
- Order: about 10:00 Reuter signalled the fleet to stand by. "At about 11:20 the flag signal was sent", referring to Paragraph Eleven [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Scuttling at Scapa Flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scuttling_of_the_German_fleet_at_Scapa_Flow)
- Planning: Reuter first raised the idea with his chief of staff in January 1919. Detailed planning began in May 1919, once the likely treaty terms were known. Orders went out on 18 June, and two transports leaving that day took away all but the reliable men [SRC]. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scuttling_of_the_German_fleet_at_Scapa_Flow)
- Done in advance: "portholes were loosened, watertight doors and condenser covers left open, and holes bored through some bulkheads so water could spread quickly" [SRC]. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scuttling_of_the_German_fleet_at_Scapa_Flow)
- Done on the signal: "seacocks and flood valves were opened, internal water pipes smashed, and drain valves on sewage tanks opened". One commander recorded that the seacocks had been "set on a hair trigger and lubricated, with large hammers placed beside valves" [SRC]. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scuttling_of_the_German_fleet_at_Scapa_Flow)
- First visible sign: "Nothing was visible until about noon, when Friedrich der Grosse began to list heavily to starboard" [SRC]. That is about 40 minutes after the order. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scuttling_of_the_German_fleet_at_Scapa_Flow)
- Casualties: nine Germans were killed and about 16 wounded by guards [SRC]. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scuttling_of_the_German_fleet_at_Scapa_Flow)
- Salvage: Cox's company raised 26 destroyers, two battlecruisers and five battleships. Alloa / Metal Industries raised five more before WWII. The remaining wrecks lie at up to 47 m [SRC]. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scuttling_of_the_German_fleet_at_Scapa_Flow)

A second list (nqht), which says it follows "the times of sinking" Reuter noted, is used to check them: [nqht "High Seas Fleet Surrender 41 – Scuttle"](https://nqht2020.duckdns.org/?p=6243). The two lists disagree for Kaiser, Prinzregent Luitpold and Dresden.

### Inferences
- [INFERRED] For the 20 large ships with times, the median from order to sinking is about 2 h 40 min, and the spread is 56 to 340 minutes. Size does not predict the order. Friedrich der Grosse (~24,700 t) went first; Hindenburg (~26,900 t) went last; the small cruiser Karlsruhe took 4.5 h. What drove the time was how well each crew had prepared and how many openings they made. For the game, model scuttling time as openings × flow, not as a function of tonnage.
- [INFERRED] Roughly 40 minutes passed between opening the valves and the first visible list on Friedrich der Grosse. A scuttled ship gives little outward sign for the first part of the process. That window is when guards, boarding parties or tugs can still save her.
- [INFERRED] Ships were saved where help was close. Baden and three light cruisers were towed or beached, and about 18-24 of the 50 destroyers were beached. With guard boats at hand, roughly one in three destroyers was saved, against 4 of the 24 large ships. Small ships near shore are easy to tow or ground.
- [EST] Inflow rate implied by the times. If a big ship has to take in roughly 10,000-20,000 t to sink (Lützow sank with about 8,000 t aboard and her bow already down, see Q3), then Friedrich der Grosse's 56 minutes means about 10,000-20,000 t/h through all her openings. Hindenburg's 340 minutes means about 2,000-3,500 t/h. The prepared openings worked at roughly 2,000-20,000 t/h in total.
- [UNCERTAIN, unverified this session] How they went, from salvage accounts: many capsized and were raised bottom-up (Kaiser class, Moltke, Seydlitz, Bayern, Derfflinger). Hindenburg settled upright in shallow water with her upperworks above the surface, which made her salvage awkward. Captions in the nqht source support "Seydlitz capsized" and "Hindenburg resting on the bottom" [SRC captions].

### Gaps
- No destroyer sinking times were found. The next places to look are the Canmore records, van der Vat's *The Grand Scuttle* and George's *Jutland to Junkyard*.
- Dresden's time conflicts: 13:50 (Wikipedia) against 15:30 (nqht). The two lists also differ for Kaiser and Prinzregent Luitpold.
- Each ship's valve list (number and size of sea cocks, the condensers' main inlets) is not in the sources read.

## Q2. Russo-Japanese War cases (1904-05)

### Takeaway
Russian scuttlings were done by opening the Kingston valves ("open the kingstons"), sometimes with charges, and mostly in shallow water. As a result Japan raised Varyag, Novik, Poltava, Retvizan and the other Port Arthur ships. The one deep-water scuttling, Sevastopol in 55 m with her sea cocks opened on one side, was the only Port Arthur battleship never raised. At sea, Admiral Ushakov sank within 40 minutes of her surrender refusal.

### Cited Findings
- Varyag (6,500 t protected cruiser, damaged, Chemulpo, 9 Feb 1904): she and Koreets "returned to harbor by 1:00 p.m., the crew decided to scuttle both ships". The British, French and Italian cruisers took off her crew. 31 were dead and 191 wounded out of 570. Japan "raised the badly damaged wreck... and commissioned it ... as the cruiser Soya on 9 July 1907" [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Varyag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_cruiser_Varyag_(1899)) The page gives no method or sinking time.
- Sevastopol (Poltava-class battleship, ~11,000 t, Port Arthur, 2 Jan 1905): Essen "opened the seacocks on one side" so that the Japanese could not salvage her. She "sank in 55 m (180 ft) of water" outside the entrance and was "the only battleship that was not salvaged by the Japanese at Port Arthur" [SRC]. Earlier, behind nets and booms, 80 torpedoes were fired at her and four hit; three of those struck the nets [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Sevastopol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_battleship_Sevastopol_(1895))
- Poltava was "also scuttled at Port Arthur and re-floated" as Tango [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Sevastopol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_battleship_Sevastopol_(1895))
- Retvizan (~12,900 t battleship) was not scuttled. She was "sunk in shallow water by thirteen 28-centimetre (11 in) howitzer shells on 6 December 1904", raised on 22 Sept 1905, renamed Hizen, and repaired at Sasebo until Nov 1908 [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Retvizan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_battleship_Retvizan)
- Novik (~3,000 t cruiser, damaged, Korsakov, Aug 1904): she took "five hits, three of them under the water line", and von Schultz ordered her scuttled, "intending to make salvage impossible". Japanese salvage began in August 1905 and "took almost a year". She was commissioned as Suzuya on 20 Aug 1906 [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Novik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_cruiser_Novik_(1900))
- Rurik (~10,900 t armoured cruiser, damaged, Ulsan, 14 Aug 1904): her steering compartment was flooded and her helm jammed to port. Lt Ivanov ordered her scuttled rather than surrender, and about 625 survivors were picked up [SRC]. The page gives no method or timing. [Wikipedia: Rurik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_cruiser_Rurik_(1892))
- Dmitri Donskoi (~6,200 t armoured cruiser, damaged, Tsushima): about 60 killed and 120 wounded. Her men were landed on Ulleungdo, and she was scuttled "the next morning, 29 May", about 1.5 nmi offshore. The page gives no method or duration [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Dmitri Donskoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_cruiser_Dmitrii_Donskoi)
- Admiral Ushakov (~4,600 t coast-defence battleship, Tsushima, 28 May 1905): Miklukha refused to surrender; "The ship was sunk within forty minutes" [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Admiral Ushakov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_battleship_Admiral_Ushakov) The page does not say whether gunfire or scuttling did more of it.

### Inferences
- [UNCERTAIN, unverified this session] Common accounts of Varyag: kingstons opened about 13:00-14:00, she lay over on her port side at about 18:10, and at low tide she was partly above water. The kingstons were used instead of charges so as not to harm foreign ships nearby. Koreets (~1,200 t gunboat) was blown up with charges at about 16:00. The steamer Sungari was set on fire and sunk. Japan raised Varyag in August 1905. Check all of this in the Russian sources.
- [UNCERTAIN, unverified this session] Ushakov was scuttled with charges and kingstons while under fire from Iwate and Yakumo, and capsized. Nakhimov and Monomakh were scuttled off Tsushima Island next morning after torpedo damage. Svetlana was sunk in action. Izumrud ran aground at Vladimir Bay, and her crew blew her up.
- [INFERRED] Shallow-harbour scuttling made salvage likely: Varyag, Novik, Poltava and Port Arthur. For the game, a scuttling in water shallower than the ship's depth should leave a raisable wreck. The enemy gets her back unless charges have also wrecked the hull or machinery.

### Gaps
- Times from start to sinking for Varyag, Koreets, Rurik, Novik and Donskoi: not in the English pages. The likely sources are the Russian official history (Работа исторической комиссии) and ru.wikipedia, which failed to load.
- Guangjia at the Yalu (1894): not researched; no time left.

## Q3. Santiago 1898 and First World War cases (Jutland, raiders, Tsingtao, Moon Sound, Novorossiysk)

### Takeaway
Three patterns appear. Charges (Dresden, Cormoran, Königsberg) sink a ship in under about 30 minutes. Torpedoes from a consort into a ship already flooding finish her in minutes: Lützow went 2 minutes after being submerged to her bridge, Svobodnaya Rossiya in 4 minutes, Rostock in about 30 minutes with charges and three torpedoes. Valves alone are slow and can be undone: the Americans pulled Cristóbal Colón off the rocks, and only then did she capsize. Torpedoes were unreliable in this role. Slava: one of six worked. Kerch needed four torpedoes.

### Cited Findings
- Cristóbal Colón (~6,800 t armoured cruiser, intact but chased down, 3 July 1898): beached at 13:15 at the mouth of the Turquino River. The captain ordered his men "to open the valves to scuttle the cruiser". That night a salvage team from USS Vulcan towed her off the rocks, then "realized she lacked watertight integrity and [she] quickly capsized and sank, a total loss" [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Cristóbal Colón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_cruiser_Crist%C3%B3bal_Col%C3%B3n)
- The other Spanish ships beached themselves while burning. Infanta María Teresa at about 10:25, Almirante Oquendo five minutes later, Vizcaya at 11:06. They were "catching fire and grounding themselves before their magazines could explode" [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Cristóbal Colón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_cruiser_Crist%C3%B3bal_Col%C3%B3n)
- Lützow (~26,700 t battlecruiser, badly damaged, 1 June 1916): about 8,000 t of water aboard by 02:20, and her forward draught over 17 m against 9.2 m normal. The bow was so deep "that the propellers were pulled partially out of the water". The order to abandon ship came at about 02:20, and six men were left trapped in the bow. "G38 fired two torpedoes into the ship". At about 02:45 she was "submerged up to her bridge" and "two minutes later she disappeared" [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Lützow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_L%C3%BCtzow) The brief named 4 torpedoes; Wikipedia says 2 [UNCERTAIN, conflict].
- Rostock (~4,900 t light cruiser, torpedoed at Jutland): "Scuttling charges had been set". V71 and V73 fired three torpedoes "to ensure Rostock sank faster". She sank bow first at about 04:25, about half an hour after Dublin appeared at about 03:55 [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Rostock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Rostock)
- Elbing (~4,400 t light cruiser, rammed by Posen): both engine rooms flooded, "not in danger of sinking". S53 took off 477 men at 02:00. A skeleton crew rigged a sail. About 03:00 British destroyers were sighted and "the order to scuttle the ship was given". The crew left in the cutter and were picked up at about 07:00 [SRC]. The page gives no method or sinking time. [Wikipedia: Elbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Elbing)
- Dresden (~3,600 t light cruiser, under fire, Más a Tierra, 14 March 1915): after a parley and a white flag, the crew prepared her for scuttling. A charge "detonated in the bow and exploded the forward ammunition magazines" at 10:45. A second charge went off in the engine rooms as she settled. She sank "in about half an hour". Her bow was torn off when it struck the seabed, she "rolled over to starboard", and she lies at 70 m [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Dresden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Dresden_(1907))
- Königsberg (~3,400 t light cruiser, wrecked by monitors, Rufiji, 11 July 1915): "At around 14:00, Looff ordered her scuttled with a torpedo" [SRC]. Her ten 105 mm guns were recovered afterwards and used ashore until 1917 [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Rufiji Delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Rufiji_Delta)
- Cormoran (II) (ex-Ryazan, ~7,000 GRT auxiliary cruiser, interned, Guam, 7 April 1917): "the Germans had secreted an explosive device in the ship's coal bunker". "Minutes after the Americans left, an explosion ... hurled debris across the harbor". Seven of about 370 died. She lies at 34 m on her starboard side [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Cormoran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Cormoran_(1909))
- Tsingtao 1914: Cormoran (I), Iltis and Luchs were scuttled 28 Sept, S90 on 17 Oct, Tiger on 29 Oct, Kaiserin Elisabeth on 2 Nov, and Jaguar on 7 Nov, the day of surrender. Kaiserin Elisabeth's guns had been landed earlier as "Batterie Elisabeth". No methods are given [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Kaiserin Elisabeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Kaiserin_Elisabeth)
- Slava (~13,500 t battleship, damaged, Moon Sound, 17 Oct 1917): about 1,130 t of water forward, a list of 8° cut to 4° by counterflooding, and a draught of about 9.8 m forward. That was too deep for the dredged channel. She was ordered scuttled at its entrance but grounded on a shoal. After the crew left, the after 12-inch magazine exploded at 11:58. Three destroyers were ordered to torpedo her; "Only one of their six torpedoes worked". She "settled on the shallow bottom" and was scrapped by Estonia in 1935 [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Slava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_battleship_Slava)
- Svobodnaya Rossiya (ex-Imperatritsa Ekaterina Velikaya, ~22,600 t dreadnought, intact, Novorossiysk, June 1918): "scuttled on 19 June by four torpedoes fired by the destroyer Kerch". "The fourth torpedo caused a massive explosion". "She capsized and sank in four minutes" [SRC]. The page's own date conflicts (infobox 18 June, text 19 June). [Wikipedia: Imperatritsa Ekaterina Velikaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_battleship_Imperatritsa_Ekaterina_Velikaya)

### Inferences
- [INFERRED] "Four minutes" for Svobodnaya Rossiya most likely runs from the fatal fourth hit, not from the first torpedo. Read it that way for the game. A ship already flooding, like Lützow, goes within minutes once torpedoes open a large new hole. An intact one may shrug off the first hits.
- [INFERRED] Charges set off a magazine (Dresden) or blow a coal bunker (Cormoran), and the ship sinks in tens of minutes at most. Valves take hours (Scapa). A ship beached and burning (Santiago) is not sunk at all, but she is wrecked as a fighting unit.
- [INFERRED] Dud torpedoes are a real risk. Slava: 1 of 6 worked. The game should roll each torpedo separately, with a high failure share for ships' torpedoes used at short range, and with a shallow-draught target in shallow water.
- [UNCERTAIN, unverified this session] The rest of Novorossiysk, 18 June 1918: the destroyers were scuttled with kingstons and charges in the roads, and Kerch was scuttled afterwards at Tuapse. Sparrowhawk (Jutland) was sunk by Marksman after her tow failed. Warrior was abandoned by Engadine while under tow and foundered; she was not scuttled. Emden (1914) was beached and wrecked on North Keeling and not scuttled. Guangjia (Yalu 1894) ran aground and was blown up by her crew.

### Gaps
- No timings found for Elbing, the Black Sea destroyers, the Civil War cases on the Danube and in the Baltic, Tsingtao, or the size of Königsberg's charge.
- No Russian-language sources were read; the pages failed to load.

## Q4. The means: Kingston valves, charges, pre-arranged orders

### Takeaway
A Kingston valve is a sea cock built so that sea pressure holds it shut. Opened from inside, it lets the sea into piping and tanks, and it was the standard tool of a scuttling. No source read gave a valve size or flow rate. Scapa shows what preparation makes possible: openings set beforehand, together with condensers, pipes and doors left open, sank capital ships in 1-6 hours. In the cases read, the Germans had charges ready (Dresden, Rostock, Cormoran). Reuter's Scapa orders were written days in advance and fired by a coded signal.

### Cited Findings
- The Kingston valve, introduced in 1837 by John Kingston, "is arranged so that, under normal operating conditions, sea pressure keeps the valve closed". It can be opened from inside to admit seawater, and "Kingston valves can be used to scuttle" a ship. The examples given are Sevastopol and Potemkin (1905), Scapa Flow (1919) and Tosa (1925) [SRC]. The article gives no sizes or flow rates. [Wikipedia: Kingston valve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingston_valve)
- Scapa's means beyond the sea cocks: condenser covers, watertight doors, loosened portholes, bulkheads bored through, internal water pipes smashed, sewage tank drains opened, and valves pre-greased with hammers beside them [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Scapa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scuttling_of_the_German_fleet_at_Scapa_Flow)
- Pre-arranged orders: Reuter's orders of 18 June were carried out on a flag signal referring to "Paragraph Eleven" [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Scapa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scuttling_of_the_German_fleet_at_Scapa_Flow)
- Charges held ready: Rostock had "Scuttling charges ... set" before the crew left [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Rostock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Rostock) Dresden had charges forward and in the engine rooms [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Dresden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Dresden_(1907)) Cormoran's device was hidden in a coal bunker before the Americans came aboard [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Cormoran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Cormoran_(1909))
- One-sided flooding to stop salvage: Essen opened the sea cocks on one side of Sevastopol [SRC]. [Wikipedia: Sevastopol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_battleship_Sevastopol_(1895))

### Inferences
- [EST] Flow through an opening below the waterline: Q = Cd · A · √(2 g h), with Cd ≈ 0.6.
  - A 300 mm valve (A = 0.071 m²) at 8 m depth passes about 0.53 m³/s, roughly 1,900 t/h.
  - A 500 mm main condenser inlet at 6 m passes about 1.3 m³/s, roughly 4,800 t/h.
  The valve sizes are guesses [UNCERTAIN]. A dreadnought's main circulating inlets are of that order: several per ship, plus many smaller sea cocks. Opening one main inlet and a few sea cocks gives 5,000-10,000 t/h. That is consistent with Scapa's fastest (about 1 h) and slowest (about 5-6 h) sinkings, if the slow ships opened fewer openings or had their doors shut [INFERRED].
- [EST] Preparation times for the game:
  - Valves alone, crew in place: a few minutes to open, plus the time to sink, 1-6 h for a capital ship.
  - Charges already prepared: minutes to set and fire. Dresden's crew finished between the parley and 10:45. Sinking takes 10-30 min.
  - Charges not prepared: an hour or more [UNCERTAIN].
  - Torpedoes from a consort: minutes to close and fire, with each torpedo failing perhaps 30-80% of the time in these cases (Slava 5 of 6 failed; Kerch needed 4).
- [EST] Where she settles: water shallower than her beam means she lands on her side or on the bottom with upperworks showing, and is raisable. In deep water she is gone. Sea cocks opened on one side give a list and a likely capsize.

### Gaps
- No primary source on valve diameters or flow rates (Kingston, condenser main inlets) for ships of 1890-1925. Ship's books and engine-room manuals would have them, for example Breyer's or Gröner's machinery details.
- No source on whether the Kaiserliche Marine kept standing scuttling charges aboard as a matter of course in 1914-18 (Sprengpatronen or Versenkungsladungen in the regulations). The cases suggest ships had them ready, but no regulation was found.
- Russian standing orders on scuttling, and the Morskoi Ustav rule against surrender, were not researched.
