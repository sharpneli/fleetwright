# What the victor did with a struck, abandoned or helpless enemy ship, 1890-1925, and how long it held his ships

Scope note: 19 tool calls. Wikipedia ship and battle pages were the main reachable sources; several fetches failed (Gneisenau, Bedovy, a Russian trial account), and no primary source (Corbett's RJW volumes, the Japanese official history, the Jutland Despatches) was read in full. Figures are tagged [SRC] (stated by the cited page), [INFERRED] (worked out from sourced figures), [EST] or [UNCERTAIN]. Items known from memory but not confirmed this session are listed under Gaps, marked "unverified", so the writer can tell them apart.

## 1. Prizes: prize crews, boarding, what they did

### Takeaway
Ships were taken as prizes when they surrendered whole and afloat: Nebogatov's four at Tsushima, Bedovy, Spartak and Avtroil in 1918. In each case the victor took the surrender only once the enemy ship had **stopped her engines and shown a signal he understood**. The prize was then taken home, under her own steam with an escort or in tow, over one to two days. A ship whose crew scuttled her (Cristóbal Colón) was lost even when the victor tried to save her. The ones the winner kept for good were salvaged later in harbour, which falls outside a battle's timescale. I found no sourced size for any prize crew.

### Cited Findings
- **The surrender at Tsushima, 28 May 1905.** The Russian remnants were sighted at 05:23. Tōgō's battleships surrounded Nebogatov and opened fire at 12,000 m. Nebogatov hoisted the surrender signal XGE, but the Japanese kept firing because their codebook had no entry for "surrender". A white cloth was then raised, and Tōgō read it as a request for a parley "since the ships were still moving". His officers found that the code required a surrendering ship to stop her engines, so firing went on while the reply "STOP" was hoisted. Nebogatov lowered the St Andrew's Cross, raised the Japanese flag and stopped all engines, and only then did Tōgō cease fire. Capture time 10:30 [SRC]. — [Battle of Tsushima, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Tsushima)
- Nebogatov decided to surrender at 10:30 after the attack began about 10:00: about 30 min under fire [SRC]. — [Russian battleship Oryol, Wikipedia (Campbell 1978; Forczyk 2009)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_battleship_Oryol)
- **Izumrud** was present at the surrender, turned south-east and fled while it was going on. She ran aground on the night of 29 May and was destroyed by her own crew [SRC]. So a ship of a surrendering force could slip away during the delay before the victor closed. — [Battle of Tsushima, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Tsushima)
- **Oryol's passage**: listing to starboard, engines failing, she followed the Japanese First Division home, escorted by Asahi and Asama and "occasionally towed" by them. She was diverted to Maizuru for emergency repairs that lasted until 29 July, and was renamed Iwami on 6 June [SRC]. 43 killed and about 80 wounded in the battle [SRC]. — [Russian battleship Oryol, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_battleship_Oryol)
- Seniavin and Apraksin were "captured as prizes of war" and became Mishima and Okinoshima, commissioned 6 June 1905 [SRC]. No prize-crew details in the source. — [Admiral Seniavin, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_coast_defense_ship_Admiral_Seniavin)
- **Bedovy** (Byedoviy), with Rozhestvensky and his staff aboard, was engaged by the destroyer Sazanami at about 15:25 on 28 May. She stopped and raised a white flag, and was captured in the late afternoon. **Akashi towed her into Sasebo on the morning of 30 May** [SRC]: a tow of roughly 1.5 days [INFERRED]. — [Battle of Tsushima, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Tsushima)
- **Sissoi Veliky**, torpedoed in the stern in the night, surrendered to the auxiliary cruisers Shinano Maru and Tainan Maru at 08:15 on 28 May and sank at 10:05. The prize was lost 1 h 50 min after the surrender [SRC]. The page's table says she sank, but its text says she was scuttled: the source contradicts itself. — [Battle of Tsushima, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Tsushima)
- **Cristóbal Colón, Santiago, 3 July 1898.** Hit six times in a 50-mile stern chase, her captain gave up when the range closed to 2,000 yd. She was beached at the Turquino River at 13:15, and he ordered the valves opened so she could not be captured. That night a salvage team from the repair ship USS Vulcan towed her off the rocks without noticing the scuttling. Lacking watertight integrity, she "quickly capsized and sank, a total loss" [SRC]. Other survivors were taken off the wreck by American sailors in small boats, while Cuban insurgents fired on Spanish survivors ashore [SRC]. — [Spanish cruiser Cristóbal Colón, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_cruiser_Crist%C3%B3bal_Col%C3%B3n)
- **Spartak, 26 December 1918.** Under fire from Wakeful from about 12:20, she ran aground on the Divel shoal and hoisted a white flag at 12:45: 25 min after fire opened [SRC]. Thesiger put a boarding party aboard (size not given) [SRC]. She was leaking badly with her propellers and rudder torn off, and Vendetta towed her back to Reval [SRC]. At anchor she was still taking water. Her crew wanted to hold a ship's Soviet meeting before raising steam for the pumps, and armed Royal Marines persuaded them to raise it. The prize crew had to make the prisoners work their own ship's pumps [SRC]. — [warhistory.org, "Destroy at all costs", Dec 1918](https://warhistory.org/2020/12/02/destroy-at-all-costs-december-1918/)
- **Avtroil, 27 December 1918.** Chased through several British ships, she was fired on by Caradoc at 11:35 and Calypso at 11:50, and hoisted a white flag about 10 min later, surrounded by five RN ships. A prize crew took her to Reval [SRC]. — [warhistory.org](https://warhistory.org/2020/12/02/destroy-at-all-costs-december-1918/)

### Inferences
- **The convention a game should model**: a ship that wants to surrender must stop her engines and show a signal the enemy can read (the enemy's flag or the international code, a white flag, the ensign hauled down). Until she does, she remains a target. Tsushima shows the delay is minutes, with firing going on through it. Emden shows the same rule (section 3). [INFERRED from Tsushima and Cocos]
- **From the surrender to the prize under way**: Tsushima, a 10:30 surrender with the ships steaming for Japan the same day. Spartak, a white flag at 12:45, a boarding party and a tow the same afternoon. So a few hours, not days, for a prize to be boarded and moving [INFERRED].
- **Speed and time home**: Bedovy was 28 May afternoon to 30 May morning in tow. The cripples (Oryol) went at a crawl and were towed at times, which held big ships (Asahi, Asama) as escorts for the whole passage [INFERRED].
- A prize crew's job, from these cases: stop the scuttling (Colón shows what happens if nobody does), keep the pumps going (Spartak), and keep the prisoners in hand (the Marines). Steering was usually left to the ship's own crew under guard, but this is [UNCERTAIN].

### Gaps
- **No sourced prize-crew size** for any Tsushima ship, Spartak or Avtroil. From memory, unverified: Japanese prize crews were on the order of a few officers and some dozens of men a ship. Corbett's *Maritime Operations in the Russo-Japanese War* vol. II and the Japanese official history (Meiji 37-38 Kaisen-shi) should give them.
- No source read on how long boarding took by boat in a seaway.
- The Weihaiwei ships of February 1895, from memory, unverified: Zhenyuan, taken over and becoming Chin'en, and others surrendered with the base under the capitulation. This was a harbour surrender, not a sea one.
- Ships the Japanese raised later, from memory, unverified: Retvizan → Hizen, Peresvet → Sagami, Poltava → Tango, Pobeda → Suwo, Pallada → Tsugaru, Bayan → Aso at Port Arthur in 1905; Varyag → Soya at Chemulpo. These are salvage over months, not battle prizes.
- Kingani, Lake Tanganyika, 26 Dec 1915, from memory, unverified: taken by the motor boats Mimi and Toutou after a short action, about 10 min. Her surviving crew surrendered. She was brought in, sank at her moorings, and was raised and recommissioned as Fifi. Not fetched.
- Whether Russian crews opened the Kingstons or wrecked gear before the Japanese boarded on 28 May (Nebogatov's trial record) was not established; the fetch failed.

## 2. Tows: who, how fast, how long, how many lost

### Takeaway
Tows of cripples were common, slow and often failed. Warrior was towed through the night by a seaplane carrier and abandoned at 08:25 the next morning. Oryol was towed at times on the way to Maizuru. Bedovy made a tow of about 1.5 days. Spartak was towed in with her screws and rudder gone. I found no sourced tow speeds.

### Cited Findings
- **Warrior (Jutland)** was taken in tow by the seaplane tender Engadine, which took off her surviving crew of 743. She was abandoned at 08:25 on 1 June with her upper deck 4 ft (1.2 m) above the water, and foundered afterwards [SRC]. The page gives no tow start time or speed. — [HMS Warrior (1905), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Warrior_(1905))
- **Oryol** was "occasionally towed" by Asahi and Asama on the passage to Japan [SRC]. — [Oryol, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_battleship_Oryol)
- **Bedovy** was towed by the cruiser Akashi from the late afternoon of 28 May into Sasebo on the morning of 30 May [SRC]. — [Battle of Tsushima, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Tsushima)
- **Spartak** was towed to Reval by Vendetta, a destroyer towing a destroyer [SRC]. — [warhistory.org](https://warhistory.org/2020/12/02/destroy-at-all-costs-december-1918/)
- **Cristóbal Colón**: a salvage tow off the beach capsized her quickly, because her valves were open [SRC]. A scuttled ship is lost the moment she is moved. — [Cristóbal Colón, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_cruiser_Crist%C3%B3bal_Col%C3%B3n)

### Inferences
- **Lost in tow** among the cases found: Warrior (abandoned after about 12 h, [INFERRED] from an evening tow to the 08:25 abandonment), Colón (capsized in the salvage tow). **Brought in**: Bedovy, Spartak, and Oryol under her own steam with help. These are too few cases for a rate.
- **Who towed whom**: whatever was at hand. A seaplane carrier (Engadine) towed an armoured cruiser, a cruiser (Akashi) a destroyer, a destroyer (Vendetta) a destroyer, battleships and armoured cruisers (Asahi, Asama) a battleship [SRC as listed]. A tow ties up the tug and usually an escort until port.

### Gaps
- **Tow speeds**, from memory, unverified: Engadine towed Warrior at a few knots (perhaps about 6-8 kn at first, slowing). Battle-damaged big-ship tows ran at about 3-6 kn generally [EST]. The Warrior tow's start time is about 20:40-21:00 on 31 May, from memory and unverified. The Jutland Despatches, Engadine's report, would settle it.
- Not researched for lack of budget: friendly comparisons such as Seydlitz's passage home (salvage ships, stern first) and Lützow (abandoned and torpedoed by her own boat G38 after her crew was taken off), and the British towing of German ships.

## 3. Finishing her off deliberately

### Takeaway
A beaten enemy that was still afloat and had not surrendered was usually finished at close range, by torpedo where a torpedo craft or cruiser was at hand, else by gunfire. A ship that kept her ensign up was treated as still fighting even when she was a wreck.

### Cited Findings
- **Blücher, Dogger Bank, 24 Jan 1915**: the light cruiser Aurora hit her with two torpedoes, and a later volley of seven more torpedoes was fired at point-blank range. She capsized at 13:13 and stayed afloat upside down for several minutes [SRC]. The page credits Aurora; the "Arethusa and Meteor" of the brief are not on it. — [SMS Blücher, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Bl%C3%BCcher)
- **Mainz, Heligoland Bight, 28 Aug 1914**: her rudder jammed at about 13:00, most of her guns were out by 13:20, and a torpedo from the destroyer Lydiard hit her amidships. With her engines stopped and her guns silent, the abandon order was repeated at 13:35 and she rolled over at 14:10 [SRC]: about 70 min from the hit that crippled her to her sinking [INFERRED]. — [SMS Mainz, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Mainz)
- **Emden, Cocos, 9 Nov 1914**: she was beached on North Keeling at about 11:20, and Sydney ceased fire and signalled "Emden beached and done for". Sydney then chased and dealt with the collier Buresk: Buresk was already scuttling, Sydney took off her crew and fired four shells to hasten her sinking. Sydney came back at about 16:00. Emden's ensign was still flying and her signals were unanswered or misunderstood, so Glossop fired **two salvoes into the wreck, killing 20 Germans**. The ensign was then hauled down and burned and a white sheet raised [SRC]. — [Battle of Cocos, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cocos)
- **Tsushima, the night of 27-28 May 1905**: Navarin was torpedoed four times and sank with 3 survivors of 622 (674 in the table) [SRC]. Nakhimov was torpedoed in the bow and scuttled off Tsushima the next morning [SRC]. Dmitri Donskoi was shot to a wreck in the afternoon of 28 May, survived until nightfall and was scuttled by her crew early on 29 May [SRC]. Svetlana was sunk by gunfire from Niitaka, Otowa and Murakumo at 10:50 on 28 May [SRC]. — [Battle of Tsushima, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Tsushima)
- **Wiesbaden**: the light cruisers of the 3rd and 4th LCS fired on her, and Onslow closed to 2,000 yd and hit her with a torpedo below the conning tower, but she stayed afloat [SRC]. — [SMS Wiesbaden, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Wiesbaden)

### Inferences
- **The convention, as practised**: a ship that has not visibly struck is still a combatant. Sydney fired on a beached Emden 4.5 h after she grounded because her ensign still flew. Tōgō fired on a surrendering squadron until it stopped [SRC for both]. Once a clear surrender is seen, firing stops [INFERRED].
- **Torpedo for the coup de grâce**: torpedoes were the usual finisher at close range (Blücher, Mainz, Wiesbaden, the Tsushima night). Several did not sink the ship at once: Wiesbaden floated about 7 h after Onslow's hit, and Blücher took more than two [SRC/INFERRED]. A game should let a finishing torpedo fail and need a second.
- **Scuttling by her own crew** was the usual end where the victor did not finish the ship (Nakhimov, Monomakh, Donskoi, Colón, Buresk) [SRC]. So the victor's "finish" was often unnecessary [INFERRED].

### Gaps
- The Falklands (Scharnhorst, Gneisenau, Nürnberg and Leipzig finished by gunfire while their crews scuttled) were not confirmed this session; the Gneisenau page failed to load.
- No written rule or order was found on when to sink a struck enemy rather than take her. The practice above is inferred from cases.

## 4. Leaving her

### Takeaway
A helpless ship was left wherever other enemy ships, night, or the threat of torpedoes and submarines made staying costly. Wiesbaden lay disabled between the battle fleets for about seven hours. Neither side took her off or finished her, and she sank in the night with one survivor.

### Cited Findings
- **Wiesbaden** was disabled by an Invincible hit in her engine room at about 18:30 on 31 May. The British line's heavy fire "prevented evacuation of the ship's crew". German torpedo boats of III Flotilla tried to rescue her crew shortly after 20:00 and were driven off by the British battle line, and a second attempt could not find her. She sank between 01:45 and 02:45 on 1 June: 589 killed and **one survivor, picked up by a Norwegian steamer the next day** [SRC]. That is about 7-8 h helpless [INFERRED]. — [SMS Wiesbaden, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Wiesbaden)
- **Emden**: Sydney left the beached wreck twice, first to chase Buresk and then for Direction Island overnight. She sent a boat with medical supplies and word that she would return the next day [SRC]. The victor left a beached enemy alone for about a day before the rescue [INFERRED: 11:20 on 9 Nov to 13:00 on 10 Nov]. — [Battle of Cocos, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cocos)
- **The Admiralty's order of 23:00, 22 Sept 1914**, after the loss of Aboukir, Hogue and Cressy: "the disabled ship must be left to her fate" (ADM 137/47) [SRC]. — [The National Archives blog, "The live bait squadron"](https://blog.nationalarchives.gov.uk/live-bait-squadron/)

### Inferences
- Reasons a ship was left, as the cases show them: **enemy fire** (Wiesbaden between the lines), **night** (Wiesbaden's second rescue attempt lost her in the dark), **other tasks** (Sydney after Buresk and the shore party), **submarines** (the 1914 order). [INFERRED from the cases]
- For the game: a helpless enemy that nobody can safely reach should be **leavable**. She keeps sinking, flooding or burning on her own clock (Wiesbaden 7 h, Warrior about 12 h in tow). The fight need not stay open for her: the victor's ships take their other tasks, and she ends by flooding, by being found later, or by scuttling [INFERRED].

### Gaps
- The Yalu (1894) and Svetlana's survivors left in the water were not researched as "left" cases. Svetlana's survivors were in fact rescued (section 5).
- The British side's own decisions about Wiesbaden (whether any captain considered taking her) were not sourced.

## 5. Rescuing the enemy's survivors

### Takeaway
The victor usually did pick up the enemy's survivors once the fighting had moved on. Auxiliaries and transports did much of it at Tsushima, hundreds a ship. Rescue under threat was broken off: by L5's bombs at Blücher, and in 1914 the Admiralty forbade stopping by a disabled ship where submarines were about. A small warship alongside could take off a few hundred men in tens of minutes. A big transfer in a swell from a wreck took hours (Sydney, about 5 h).

### Cited Findings
- **Blücher**: British destroyers closed to pick up survivors. The Zeppelin L5 took the sinking Blücher for a British battlecruiser and bombed the destroyers, which withdrew [SRC]. Survivors: 234 of a crew of at least 1,200 (Goldrick, from British documents), 260 (Hildebrand, Röhr and Steinmetz), or 6 officers and 275 men (Schmalenbach) [SRC]. Beatty broke off the pursuit at 13:50 [SRC]. — [SMS Blücher, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Bl%C3%BCcher)
- **Mainz**: shortly before 14:00 the destroyer Lurcher came alongside and took off the wounded. She sank at 14:10, and the British rescued **348** men as prisoners, 89 killed [SRC]. The figures are inconsistent with the page's own complement of 367 (348 + 89 = 437), so treat the survivor count as [UNCERTAIN]. Wolfgang von Tirpitz was picked up by a boat and taken to Liverpool [SRC]. — [SMS Mainz, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Mainz)
- **Emden**: Sydney returned at 13:00 on 10 Nov and, once assured the Germans would not fight, transferred the survivors in **about five hours**, slowed by the wounded, rough seas and overcrowding. Her medical staff worked from 18:00 to 04:30 on 11 Nov. Men ashore were recovered on 11 Nov, the dark having made the beach too hard to reach the night before. On 12 Nov most prisoners went to the Empress of Russia [SRC]. 130-134 Germans killed [SRC, the page contradicts itself]. — [Battle of Cocos, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cocos)
- **Tsushima rescues** [SRC]: Svetlana, 290 men (23 wounded) rescued by the transport Amerika Maru. Sissoi Veliky, 42 officers and 571 men saved after her surrender at 08:15 and before she sank at 10:05, by Shinano Maru and Tainan Maru. Nakhimov, over 600 saved by boats, fishing boats and the armed merchant cruiser Sado Maru. Monomakh, 32 officers and 374 men by the armed merchant cruiser Manshu. Donskoi's crew rowed to Matsushima and were taken prisoner that afternoon by landing parties from Fubuki and Kasuga Maru. 6,016 Russians captured in all. — [Battle of Tsushima, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Tsushima)
- **Aboukir, Hogue and Cressy, 22 Sept 1914**: Hogue closed on the sinking Aboukir to help and stopped for some time, partly because her boats were slow to launch, and was torpedoed as she got under way. Cressy stood by and was sunk at 07:50, about 15 min after U-9's first attack on her. Before British warships came at 10:45, trawlers and two Dutch steamers (Flora, Titan) picked up many survivors. The Admiralty order followed at 23:00 [SRC]. Aboukir sank about 10 min after her torpedo, Hogue 10-15 min after hers [SRC]. — [The National Archives blog](https://blog.nationalarchives.gov.uk/live-bait-squadron/)

### Inferences (rates for the game)
- **Two auxiliaries alongside a stopped, surrendered ship**: 613 men in at most 110 min (Sissoi Veliky, 08:15-10:05), about 330 men an hour, roughly **170 men a ship-hour** [INFERRED]. It was calm enough for the ship to float the whole time.
- **One destroyer alongside a sinking cruiser**: Lurcher took off the wounded in roughly 10-15 min before Mainz rolled over. The rest of the 348 came out of the water, by boats from several ships [INFERRED from the timings; the per-ship split is not sourced].
- **A cruiser's boats from a wreck through surf and swell**: about 5 h for Emden's survivors, the wounded taking longest [SRC duration]. If the survivors were around 200 (not sourced), that is roughly 40 men an hour [EST].
- **The pursuit's cost**: rescue held the rescuers out of the fight for its duration. At Dogger Bank the destroyers left under air attack, and the pursuit had ended by 13:50 for other reasons [SRC]. In 1914 the Admiralty judged a stopped ship by survivors to be a target, which turned rescue into a choice made under threat [SRC order; INFERRED rule].
- **Who rescues**: at Tsushima mostly the auxiliaries and transports following the fleet, leaving the battle divisions free. In the North Sea, destroyers and light cruisers, or neutral merchantmen [SRC cases; INFERRED pattern].

### Gaps
- The Falklands rescues (Inflexible and Invincible picking up Gneisenau's men; Carnarvon and Kent others) were not confirmed this session. From memory, unverified: about 190 of Gneisenau's men were picked up from water at about 40°F (4 °C), and a number died of the cold after rescue.
- Japanese rescue times at Tsushima beyond Sissoi Veliky's window were not established.
- Rescue figures for the Yalu (1894) and Santiago (US boats taking Spanish crews off the beached wrecks) were not quantified in the sources read.

## 6. Written rules

### Takeaway
The 1907 Hague Convention X binds the belligerents to search for the shipwrecked and wounded after every engagement, "so far as military interests permit", and makes the rescued prisoners of war. That proviso is what let the RN's 1914 order, "the disabled ship must be left to her fate", stand beside it. I found no sourced text of the prize codes on taking a struck warship.

### Cited Findings
- **Hague Convention X (1907), Art. 16**: "After every engagement, the two belligerents, so far as military interests permit, shall take steps to look for the shipwrecked, sick, and wounded, and to protect them, as well as the dead, against pillage and ill treatment" [SRC]. **Art. 14**: the shipwrecked, wounded or sick who fall into the enemy's power are prisoners of war [SRC]. **Art. 12**: a belligerent warship may demand the delivery of sick, wounded or shipwrecked men from hospital ships, merchant ships, yachts or boats of any nationality [SRC]. **Art. 9**: neutral vessels that rescue them cannot be captured for having them on board [SRC]. **Art. 4**: hospital ships aid both sides' wounded and shipwrecked and act at their own risk during and after engagements [SRC]. — [Hague Convention X, 1907, Avalon Project](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/hague10.asp)
- **RN order of 23:00, 22 Sept 1914**: "the disabled ship must be left to her fate" (ADM 137/47) [SRC]. — [The National Archives blog](https://blog.nationalarchives.gov.uk/live-bait-squadron/)
- **The surrender signal in practice, 1905**: the Japanese signal book's surrender convention required a surrendering ship to stop her engines. A flag signal or a white cloth alone, with the ship still moving, was not accepted [SRC]. — [Battle of Tsushima, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Tsushima)

### Inferences
- The legal duty to rescue is conditional on "military interests". Both the Admiralty's 1914 order and the victor's practice (rescue once the fight had moved on, broken off under threat) fit that proviso [INFERRED].
- A game rule consistent with this: after an encounter quiets, the victor's ships may be ordered or choose to rescue. The AI weighs this against threats in its picture (submarines, aircraft, enemy ships, night) and against its pursuit [INFERRED].

### Gaps
- Not read: the US Naval War Code of 1900, the German Prisenordnung of 1909, the RN's Prize Manual, Hague XI (restrictions on capture) and Hague XIII. These mostly govern merchant prizes. Whether any of them prescribes the handling of a surrendered warship (prize crew, destination, prize court) was not established.
- No written order was found on pursuing rather than picking up survivors, beyond the Admiralty's 1914 order. Jellicoe's and Beatty's Grand Fleet Battle Orders (GFBOs) might hold one; unverified.
